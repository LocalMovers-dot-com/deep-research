## Prompt 1

﻿# Manufactured home transport costs in the San Antonio metro, 2026

**Moving a manufactured home in the San Antonio–New Braunfels metro area costs between $5,000 and $35,000 depending on home type, distance, and service scope.** A single-wide local full-service move runs **$5,000–$8,000**, a double-wide **$10,000–$16,000**, and a triple-wide **$15,000–$25,000** — but those ranges exclude skirting, steps, utility hookups, and inspections. When every dollar a homeowner actually spends is tallied, realistic all-in costs climb to **$8,500–$13,000** for a single-wide, **$15,000–$22,000** for a double-wide, and **$22,000–$35,000** for a triple-wide on a sub-50-mile move. This analysis draws on TxDMV permit schedules, TDHCA fee rules, FMCSA carrier records, Census housing data, and pricing from ProMatcher, HomeAdvisor/Angi, HomeGuide, CostHelper, and multiple Texas-based transporters to produce the most granular cost picture available for this market.

## Transport-only versus full-service pricing for local moves under 50 miles

The critical distinction in manufactured home moving is between **transport-only** (tow truck hauls the structure from point A to point B) and **full-service** (disconnect, transport, reconnect, block, level, anchor, skirt, and finish). Most national cost guides conflate the two, producing confusingly wide ranges.

### Single-wide (14 × 56 to 16 × 80 ft)

| Metric | Transport-only | Full-service |
|---|---|---|
| Low estimate | $1,500 | $5,000 |
| High estimate | $5,000 | $8,000 |
| **Median** | $3,000 | **$6,500** |
| **Mean** | $3,200 | **$6,500** |

ProMatcher reports a San Antonio–area (ZIP 78284) transport-only average of **$2,463** with a range of $2,200–$2,726, though that data originates from July 2018 and understates current pricing by roughly 25–35%. HomeAdvisor's March 2025 national data centers the single-wide full-service average at **$6,000**, while Texas-specific 2025 firsthand reports on Quora place full-service single-wide moves at **$5,000–$8,000** within a 50-mile radius. CostHelper's 2024 data cites $3,000–$5,000 for full-service short-distance single-wides, but this appears to reflect lower-cost rural markets, not San Antonio.

### Double-wide (two 14–16 ft sections, 28 × 48 to 32 × 80 ft)

| Metric | Transport-only | Full-service |
|---|---|---|
| Low estimate | $3,000 | $10,000 |
| High estimate | $6,000 | $16,000 |
| **Median** | $4,500 | **$13,000** |
| **Mean** | $4,500 | **$13,500** |

Double-wides require transporting two sections on separate trucks (or in tandem), then reassembling the marriage line on-site. HomeAdvisor's national average is **$11,500**; Texas-specific data runs higher. A 2025 Quora user reported paying **$15,000** for a double-wide move within 50 miles in Texas (handling their own utility disconnect/reconnect), while another Texas user cited **$12,000–$18,000** as the going range. ProMatcher lists a San Antonio double-wide long-distance rate of **$2.50–$4.75 per mile** (transport-only, August 2022 data from Texas Statewide Mobile Home Transport, ZIP 78223).

### Triple-wide (three 14–16 ft sections)

| Metric | Transport-only | Full-service |
|---|---|---|
| Low estimate | $5,000 | $15,000 |
| High estimate | $10,000 | $25,000 |
| **Median** | $7,500 | **$20,000** |
| **Mean** | $7,500 | **$20,000** |

Triple-wides are uncommon and data is sparse. Angi's March 2026 update cites **$12,000–$25,000+** nationally. First Choice Home Center (yourfchc.com) quotes **$10,000–$14,000+** for full-service up to 60 miles. Three separate transport trips, three TxDMV permits, and two marriage-line assemblies make triple-wides roughly **50–70% more expensive** than double-wides.

## Long-distance pricing from 50 to 200+ miles

Long-distance moves layer incremental per-mile transport charges onto the same fixed setup costs. The tow truck rate runs **$4.00–$5.50 per loaded mile per section**, while pilot/escort cars (when required) add **$1.50–$2.00 per mile** each.

| Home type | 100-mile full-service | 200-mile full-service |
|---|---|---|
| Single-wide | $9,000–$16,000 | $13,000–$20,000 |
| Double-wide | $15,000–$24,000 | $20,000–$32,000 |
| Triple-wide | $22,000–$35,000 | $30,000–$45,000 |

The transport-only component for a 100-mile single-wide move is approximately **$4,000–$5,500** (versus $1,500–$3,000 for a 30-mile local move). For double-wides, the per-mile premium doubles because two sections travel independently. HomeGuide (2026) cites an all-in long-distance rate of **$6–$15 per mile**, which aligns with the blended rates calculated below.

## Conclusion: a fixed-cost-heavy market with thin carrier supply

Three findings stand out from this analysis. First, **fixed costs dwarf transport charges** in manufactured home moves under 50 miles — foundation work, skirting, steps, and utility reconnection account for 60–70% of a typical all-in local move cost, while the actual trucking is 15–25%. Homeowners who focus solely on the transport quote and neglect to budget for setup and finishing will be blindsided by a bill that doubles their expectations. Second, **the San Antonio carrier market is thin**: six SAFER-verified transporters with a combined fleet of roughly 25–30 power units serve a metro area with ~48,000 manufactured housing units. This supply constraint explains the 30–45 day booking lead times and leaves little room for competitive pricing pressure. Third, **SAWS impact fees are the wild card** — at $7,343–$11,528 for a new residential water/sewer connection (as of July 2024), they can single-handedly add more to the project cost than the transport itself. Homeowners moving to a lot with existing utility service avoid this entirely; those placing on raw land should treat it as the largest single line item in their budget.

The TxDMV permit structure is remarkably affordable — **$40 per section per trip** — and Texas's manufactured-housing-specific escort exemption (no escort required up to 16 feet wide) saves $100–$300 per move compared to states applying standard oversize-load escort rules. TDHCA installation fees ($75–$125) are similarly modest. The real costs in this market are labor and materials at the destination site, not regulatory compliance.

## Prompt 2

﻿# Manufactured home transport permitting in San Antonio: eight regulatory layers decoded

**Moving a manufactured home within, into, or out of the San Antonio metro area requires navigating at least eight overlapping regulatory frameworks — state housing authority licensing, oversize-load highway permits, escort requirements, local building permits, floodplain compliance, and potentially interstate or international clearances.** The total regulatory burden is substantial: a single intrastate move of a standard singlewide home triggers a minimum of a $40 TxDMV highway permit, a $75 TDHCA installation-reporting fee, and a $78 City of San Antonio installation permit — plus the installer's $25,000 surety bond and $300,000 liability insurance must already be in place. A doublewide crossing from Louisiana adds separate per-section permits in each state. This guide details every layer with statutory citations, current fee amounts, processing timelines, and filing portals.

---

## 1. TDHCA manufactured housing division: the state licensing foundation

The Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs governs manufactured home installation through **Texas Administrative Code Title 10, Part 1, Chapter 80**, with statutory authority under **Texas Occupations Code Chapter 1201**. The rules are organized across seven subchapters: Subchapter A (§§80.1–80.3) covers fees and administration; Subchapter B (§§80.20–80.26) sets installation standards; Subchapter C (§§80.30–80.38) defines licensee responsibilities; Subchapter D (§§80.40–80.41) governs licensing; and Subchapters E through G cover enforcement, consumer claims, and statements of ownership. The most recent rule amendments took effect **March 3, 2024** (49 TexReg 521), with further licensing amendments effective **July 14, 2024** (49 TexReg 4429).

**Installer license requirements** demand completion of a 12-hour education program (8-hour Core Licensing Education Course plus 4-hour Installer LEC), passage of both the Core exam and Installer exam with scores of 70% or above, a fingerprint-based criminal background check, and filing of a **$25,000 surety bond** with TDHCA. No prior experience is required. The biennial license fee is **$350**, and the education courses cost $150 (Core) plus $50 (Installer), with exam fees of $85 (Core) and $70 (Installer) paid to PSI Exams. Continuing education of 8 hours per renewal cycle is mandated under Occupations Code §1201.113. New license applications are processed in approximately **7 working days** once all materials are submitted (per TDHCA Form 1017, rev. 11/21/2023).

The installation reporting fee under TAC §80.3(b) is **$75 for the first section** and **$25 for each additional section**. The Notice of Installation (Form T) must be submitted to TDHCA within 7 days of installation completion. Statement of Ownership transactions cost **$55 each** (Form 1022, rev. 09/01/2017), processed within 15 working days per Occupations Code §1201.207(a). Salvaged home plan review and inspection costs **$200** plus mileage. Homeowners can verify any installer's license status through TDHCA's online License View portal at https://mhweb.tdhca.state.tx.us/mhweb/license_view.jsp, which is updated regularly (last confirmed update: 02/27/2026). The main manufactured housing division hub is at https://www.tdhca.texas.gov/mhd.

## 2. TxDMV oversize permits and the TxPROS routing system

Highway transport permits for manufactured homes fall under **Texas Transportation Code Chapter 623, Subchapter E (§§623.091–623.105)** — a dedicated subchapter separate from general oversize/overweight provisions. A critical jurisdictional note: **permitting authority transferred from TxDOT to the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles (TxDMV) on January 1, 2012**, though TxDOT retains control over route restrictions and engineering studies.

The **single-trip manufactured housing permit** costs **$40** (§623.096(a)), is valid for one movement not exceeding 5 days, and may be issued to TDHCA-licensed manufacturers, retailers, or installers, registered motor carriers, or homeowners whose name matches the title. The **annual manufactured housing permit** costs **$1,500** (§623.096(b)) but is limited to transporting new homes from manufacturing facilities to temporary storage within 20 miles. Both permit types are filed through **TxPROS (Texas Permitting & Routing Optimization System)** at https://txpros.txdmv.gov/ or by phone at (800) 299-1700. Standard self-issued permits process **within minutes**; complex route studies may take days to weeks.

Dimensional limits and escort thresholds are critical for San Antonio-area moves. Legal travel without a permit requires staying within **8'6" wide, 14' high, and 65' long** (combination). Under manufactured housing permits, the maximum dimensions are **20' wide, 16' high, and 110' overall length**. Escort requirements follow §623.099 exclusively — not the general oversize rules in 43 TAC §219.11(k), which explicitly exclude manufactured housing:

- **Up to 16' wide**: No escort required
- **Over 16' to 18' wide**: One escort (front on two-lane roads, rear on multi-lane highways)
- **Over 18' wide**: Two escorts, front and rear

Loads exceeding **16' wide** cannot use main lanes of controlled-access highways and must use frontage roads. The speed limit during transport is **55 mph on highways** and **45 mph on county roads/city streets** (§623.098). Transport is restricted to **daylight hours** (half-hour before sunrise to half-hour after sunset) and is **prohibited on six national holidays**: New Year's Day, Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, and Christmas.

**San Antonio-area highway restrictions are exceptionally detailed.** Per the TxDMV San Antonio District Permit Restrictions document (updated through 3/17/2026), key constraints include an **11-foot maximum width** on segments of IH-35 between Binz Engleman Road and Eisenhauer Road, a **10-foot maximum width** on US-281 between LP 13 and North IH-410, and **no oversize permits at all** on certain IH-10/LP-1604 segments during weekends. Inside Loop 410, no oversize loads are permitted on any highway **unless the vehicle has an origin or destination there** (Restriction #19802). The San Antonio District contact for permit questions is SAT.OSOW.Permits@TxDOT.gov, with specific contacts at (210) 615-5912 and (210) 615-5866.

## 3. DPS escorts are not what most people expect

A widespread misconception holds that Texas DPS troopers escort manufactured homes. In reality, **Texas DPS does not operate a formal fee-based oversize load escort program.** The escorts required under Transportation Code §623.099 are **private civilian escort flag vehicles (pilot cars)**, not law enforcement vehicles. This distinguishes Texas from states like Alabama, which runs state trooper escorts with published fee schedules.

Private escort vehicles must meet specific requirements under §623.099(c): gross vehicle weight between **1,000 and 10,000 pounds**, two flashing amber lights or one rotating amber beacon of at least 8 inches diameter, four **16-inch red square flags** mounted on the vehicle's corners, a "WIDE LOAD" sign with yellow background and black letters, and two-way radio communication with the permitted vehicle. Texas requires **pilot/escort vehicle operator (PEVO) certification** — the state accepts Washington State PEVO certification offered through the Evergreen Safety Council.

When law enforcement escorts are specifically required by a permit or local jurisdiction, they are typically arranged through **off-duty police officers** via private security companies. Market rates run approximately **$75–$150 per hour** per officer with 4-hour minimums. Companies like First Responder Protective Services (Texas DPS Private Security License #C10520601) provide this service in the San Antonio area. However, §623.099(f)–(g) contains strong preemption language: **counties and municipalities may not require escort arrangements beyond what state law specifies** for manufactured homes.

A key convoy provision allows two transportable sections of a multisection home (or two singlewide homes) towed within **1,000 feet** of each other to share escorts as if they were one unit (§623.099(d)). The towing vehicle must display four red or orange 16-inch flags on the front (§623.100), and each manufactured home must have a wiring harness providing rear taillights, brake lights, and turn signals (§623.102(b)). The TDHCA license or registration number must be affixed to the rear of the home in letters at least **8 inches high** (§623.094(b)).

## 4. Bexar County's surprisingly light regulatory hand

Unincorporated Bexar County operates under a **limited regulatory framework** for manufactured homes due to Texas Local Government Code Chapter 233, Subchapter C (§§233.061–233.067), which restricts county authority over single-family residential construction. For single-family manufactured homes (1–3 dwelling units), the county **cannot legally mandate building permits** — permitting is voluntary unless required by an HOA or a lending institution for a Certificate of Occupancy. Furthermore, **unincorporated Bexar County has no zoning**, meaning no zones prohibit manufactured homes and no county-imposed setback or lot size requirements exist (outside the City of San Antonio's Military Protection Area).

When permits are obtained voluntarily — or when commercial manufactured home parks (4+ units) are involved — the process runs through the **Bexar County Fire Marshal's Office (BCFMO)** and Public Works. A Building Permit Authorization (BPA) from Public Works at 1948 Probandt Street must be obtained first, followed by submission to the Fire Marshal at 9810 Southton Road. Applications are accepted **in person by appointment only** at (210) 335-0300 (option 2). There is **no online permitting portal** for Bexar County.

The fee schedule (effective January 1, 2024) sets **$750 flat** for a modular/mobile structure installation permit (commercial/public use), which includes plan review, installation inspection, and Certificate of Occupancy. Trade permits for electrical, plumbing, and HVAC run **$150 each** ($250 if not associated with an active building permit). Floodplain development permits cost **$50 per activity** through the Development Services Section of Public Works. State law gives the county **30 days** to review and approve or deny any voluntarily submitted building permit (§233.063).

On-site sewage facility (OSSF/septic) permits through Bexar County Environmental Services are mandatory when municipal sewer is unavailable — and critically, **no utility meters will be released for manufactured homes until the septic system is installed and permitted**. Contact Bexar County Environmental Services at (210) 335-6700 or via bexar.org/3218/On-Site-Sewage-Facilities.

## 5. San Antonio's floodplain rules add real complexity

The City of San Antonio's Development Services Department (DSD) at 1901 S. Alamo Street imposes the most detailed local requirements for manufactured home installation. Two separate permit applications exist: one for **private lot installation** and one for **placement in an established park**. Both are available through the **BuildSA online portal** at www.sa.gov/Directory/Departments/DSD/BuildSA and as PDFs at docsonline.sanantonio.gov.

The manufactured home installation permit fee is **$78 flat** (FY 2025 fee schedule, revised October 2025). Required submittals for private lot installation include a completed building permit application, a notarized tree application/affidavit, two sets of site plans matching the recorded plat, a **foundation plan stamped and sealed by a Texas Professional Engineer or Registered Architect**, floor plans, exterior elevations, and a window/door schedule. Separate trade permits are required for electrical, plumbing, mechanical, sewer/septic, and gas work. Inspections include foundation, framing, and final — all must be completed before utility release. Applications expire if no permit issues within **180 days**, with extensions available in 180-day increments.

**Zoning is the primary barrier** within San Antonio. The Unified Development Code (UDC) limits manufactured homes to three special districts: **"MH" Manufactured Housing District (§35-354)** for individual lots, **"MHC" Manufactured Housing Conventional District (§35-355)** for conventional subdivisions, and **"MHP" Manufactured Housing Park District (§35-356)** for parks. Manufactured homes are generally **prohibited in standard residential districts** (R-4, R-5, R-6, etc.). Properties must front on a paved city street per UDC §35-506(d)(9)C.3. Zoning verification is available at (210) 207-1111.

San Antonio's floodplain exposure is substantial, governed by **UDC Appendix F ("Floodplains — Areas of Special Flood Hazard")**. Key provisions under §35-F142 require that the lowest floor of any new residential structure — including manufactured homes — be elevated **one foot above the base flood elevation (BFE)**. In AO/AH zones (§35-F143), the elevation must be one foot above the regulatory floodplain, one foot above the highest adjacent grade, or one foot above the FIRM depth number — whichever is highest. The 50% rule applies: improvements exceeding 50% of a structure's market value within a 10-year period trigger substantial-improvement requirements. A Floodplain Development Permit must be signed and sealed by a registered PE. Floodplain verification is handled by Transportation & Capital Improvements at (210) 207-8047.

**Schertz** (spanning Guadalupe, Bexar, and Comal Counties) is distinctive for its **AICUZ (Air Installation Compatible Use Zone)** restrictions tied to Randolph Air Force Base, which impose unique land use constraints. The CityView Portal for online permits is at development.schertz.com/Portal/. Schertz offers Saturday office hours (9 AM–5 PM), unusual among these jurisdictions.

## 7. Interstate moves multiply the permit stack

A manufactured home crossing state lines requires **separate oversize permits from each state** — no centralized multi-state system exists. Each neighboring state has its own permit fees, dimensional limits, escort thresholds, and online portals.

**Louisiana (LaDOTD)** charges just **$10 per trip** for a mobile home oversize permit valid for 3 consecutive days, filed through the LaGeaux system at https://lageauxpm.dotd.la.gov/ or by phone at (800) 654-1433. Escorts are required for loads exceeding **12 feet wide and/or 90 feet long**. Loads wider than 12 feet are prohibited on Interstate through Shreveport, Monroe, Lake Charles, Baton Rouge, and New Orleans metro areas. A minimum **$100,000 insurance certificate** naming LaDOTD as holder is required for single trips ($250,000 for charge accounts), per Act 301 of the 2019 Regular Session. Mardi Gras restrictions prohibit travel in specific parishes during the celebration period. (Louisiana Administrative Code Title 73, Part I, Chapter 3, §I-303.)

**New Mexico (NMDOT/NMDPS)** charges **$25 per trip** for a 5-day permit, filed at https://truckpermits.dot.nm.gov/ or at ports of entry. A unique provision requires proof that **all property taxes on the manufactured home are paid** before a permit is issued for moves originating in New Mexico, with the county assessor notified of both origin and destination (N.M. Stat. Ann. §66-7-413). Maximum permitted width is an absolute **18 feet** with strict overhang limits. State Police escorts cost an additional **$300 per day** when required (loads over 20 feet wide). Self-issue permit books are available for **$250 per book of ten** for transporters/dealers. (NMAC 18.19.8, effective 2/12/2016, amended 6/1/2022.)

**Oklahoma (ODOT)** charges **$40 per single trip** for manufactured homes, matching Texas's fee (OAC §730:50-5-18, adopted September 1, 2023). Permits are filed at http://swpermitsok.com/ after establishing a company profile. Escort requirements trigger at **12 feet wide** (one escort) and **14 feet wide** (front and rear escorts). Loads **over 16 feet wide cannot use the Interstate/Defense Highway system**. Oklahoma requires specifically sized towing vehicles: a truck of at least 2 tons with rear duals for homes over 12 feet wide (OAC §595:30-3-19). Rush hour restrictions in Oklahoma City and Tulsa metro areas run 7 AM–9 AM and 3:30 PM–6:30 PM.

**Mexico border crossings** involve CBP documentation, HUD compliance, and customs brokerage. Homes imported into the U.S. must comply with **HUD Manufactured Home Construction and Safety Standards (24 CFR Part 3280)** and carry a HUD certification label — effectively requiring production in HUD-approved facilities. Exports require Electronic Export Information filing via AES for goods over $2,500. Eagle Pass's Camino Real International Bridge (Bridge II) offers a **25-foot-wide lane** suitable for manufactured home transport, while Laredo's World Trade Bridge handles the heaviest commercial volume. Mexican customs requires a Pedimento de Importación and engagement of a licensed customs broker. Under current trade policy, a **25% tariff** may apply to non-USMCA goods from Mexico per Executive Orders 14194 and 14198 (February 2025), though the tariff situation remains volatile.

All interstate moves also require **IRP (International Registration Plan) compliance** for commercial vehicles over 26,000 lbs GVW and **IFTA (International Fuel Tax Agreement)** quarterly fuel tax reporting. Without IRP registration, trip permits must be obtained for each state.

## 8. Texas carrier licensing hinges on existing TDHCA credentials

A critical finding of this analysis: **Texas does not issue a separate "transporter" license for manufactured home movers.** Under Occupations Code §1201.161(a), a licensed retailer or installer is **not required to obtain any additional permit, certificate, or license** to transport manufactured housing to the installation site, except for the TxDMV oversize permit under Transportation Code Chapter 623, Subchapter E. The TDHCA license types are manufacturer, retailer, broker, installer, and salesperson — no "transporter" category exists (§1201.003(17)).

The practical effect is that **the installer's existing TDHCA license and bond cover transportation activities**. The $25,000 installer surety bond (or $50,000 retailer bond) does not need supplementation for transport. However, the **$300,000 combined single limit liability insurance** requirement under Transportation Code §623.103 applies to **every vehicle towing a manufactured house**, including homeowner self-moves. This insurance threshold exceeds standard commercial auto minimums and represents a significant compliance requirement.

Transportation Code §623.094 limits permit eligibility to four categories: TDHCA-licensed manufacturers, retailers, or installers; registered motor carriers with USDOT numbers; homeowners named on the title who own or lease the towing vehicle; or persons authorized by the TDHCA director. TxDMV verifies USDOT numbers against FMCSA's MCMIS database for out-of-service orders before issuing any permit. Penalties for unauthorized transport are substantial: **$1,000 for a first offense** (Class C misdemeanor), escalating to $2,000 plus up to 30 days in jail for a second offense, and $5,000 plus up to one year in jail for subsequent violations (§623.104). Paying an unlicensed person to move a home carries a separate **$1,000 misdemeanor fine** (§623.105).

Section §1201.161(d) provides important preemption: **local governments may not require a separate permit, fee, bond, or insurance for transportation or installation** by a licensed retailer or installer, except for collection of actual costs incurred. This means municipal transport permits — beyond the state-level TxDMV permit — are generally preempted for TDHCA licensees.

## Conclusion: a regulatory stack that rewards preparation

The manufactured home transport and installation framework in San Antonio involves genuine regulatory complexity, but it follows a logical hierarchy. **TDHCA licensing forms the foundation** — without a valid installer or retailer license and the associated $25,000+ bond and $300,000 liability insurance, no legitimate transport can occur. **TxDMV's $40 single-trip permit through TxPROS** handles the highway authorization and routes around San Antonio's many corridor-specific width and height restrictions, particularly the tight constraints inside Loop 410. The escort system relies on **private pilot cars, not DPS troopers**, with thresholds more generous for manufactured homes (16 feet) than general oversize loads (14 feet).

Local permitting varies dramatically: unincorporated Bexar County effectively cannot require single-family manufactured home building permits, while the City of San Antonio restricts placement to three dedicated MH zoning districts and imposes rigorous floodplain elevation requirements. Adjacent cities add their own variations — New Braunfels limits MH to three zones, Kyle charges explicit moving fees with police escort provisions, and Schertz layers AICUZ military compatibility requirements. Interstate moves multiply the permit stack but at modest cost (Louisiana at just $10 per trip, New Mexico at $25, Oklahoma at $40).

The single most consequential finding for practitioners is the **state preemption principle**: both §623.099(g) (prohibiting local escort add-ons) and §1201.161(d) (prohibiting local transport licensing) establish that TDHCA-licensed operators with valid TxDMV permits operate under state authority that local jurisdictions cannot supplement with additional transport-layer requirements. Understanding this preemption — while still complying with local installation and zoning requirements at the destination — is the key to navigating the San Antonio manufactured home transport framework efficiently.

## Per-mile rate components and how they build

The building blocks of manufactured home transport pricing break down into distinct per-mile components that stack predictably. The **towing vehicle (toter truck)** charges **$4.00–$5.50 per mile**, representing the single largest variable cost. Escort/pilot car vehicles add **$1.50–$3.50 per mile per vehicle**, with high-pole escort cars (for height clearance scouting) running **$2.25–$2.50 per mile**. These rates apply per section — a double-wide requires two toter trucks and, if escorts are needed, the convoy can share escort vehicles under Texas's 1,000-foot convoy provision.

The transport-only per-mile rate for a single-wide with one escort works out to roughly **$5.50–$7.50 per mile**. For a double-wide convoyed (two sections sharing escorts), the effective rate is **$9.50–$13.00 per mile**. Industry sources commonly cite an all-in blended rate (transport + escorts + permits amortized) of **$6–$15 per mile per section** for long-distance moves, consistent with data from This Old House (2026), TX Mobile Home Buyer, and Pricing Van Lines (2025).

Additional cost components that factor into all-in pricing include prep/teardown at **$1,500–$2,500** (disconnecting utilities, removing skirting, separating sections), setup at destination at **$1,000–$5,000 per section** (leveling, blocking, anchoring, reconnection), hitch welding at **$200–$300** for single-wide or **$400–$600** for double-wide halves, and tire replacement at roughly **$50 per tire** or axle repair at **$100–$200 per axle** when needed.

## TxDMV permits: flat fees that don't scale with distance

One of the most significant cost advantages for Texas-based moves is the state's remarkably simple and inexpensive permit structure. The **TxDMV manufactured housing single-trip permit costs a flat $40 per section** — the same fee whether the move covers 5 miles or 500 miles. There is **no per-mile surcharge, no per-county fee, and no highway maintenance fee** (unless the load exceeds 80,000 lbs gross vehicle weight, which is unusual for standard manufactured homes). This was confirmed in the TxDMV May 2025 Motor Carrier Handbook and the TxDMV Table of Permit Fees.

Each section of a multi-section home requires its own permit, so total permit costs are simply:

- **Single-wide:** $40 (one section)
- **Double-wide:** $80 (two sections × $40)
- **Triple-wide:** $120 (three sections × $40)

These fees are dramatically lower than many other states' permit structures and contribute to Texas's overall transport cost advantage. For comparison, a General Single-Trip Permit (required if the load is overweight) costs $60 at legal weight, escalating to $210–$470 for heavier loads. The Annual Manufactured Housing Permit costs **$1,500/year** but is limited to factory-to-storage moves within 20 miles of the manufacturing facility — relevant mainly for factory operations near Seguin.

Permits are issued through the TxPROS online system, valid for one movement within five days. **No transport is permitted on six national holidays** (New Year's Day, Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, Christmas).

## Escort and pilot car costs by width threshold

Texas applies **manufactured-housing-specific escort thresholds** that differ from general oversize load rules, creating meaningful cost differences depending on the actual width of each section including eaves overhang.

| MH section width | Escort requirement | Estimated cost per 100 miles |
|---|---|---|
| **≤16 feet** | No escort required | $0 |
| **>16 ft to ≤18 ft** | 1 escort (front on 2-lane, rear on 4+ lane) | $150–$350 |
| **>18 feet** | 2 escorts (front and rear, all highways) | $300–$700 |

Most standard single-wide homes (14–16 ft body width) fall at or below the 16-foot threshold, meaning **no escort is required** — a significant cost savings. However, eave overhangs can push the effective transport width beyond 16 feet, triggering the one-escort requirement. Double-wide sections transported individually are typically 14–16 feet each, also often avoiding escort requirements.

Texas law allows **two sections of a multi-section home to convoy and share escorts** when the distance between units does not exceed 1,000 feet. This convoy provision can cut escort costs nearly in half for double-wide moves where both halves exceed 16 feet.

**Texas does not generally require DPS (law enforcement) escorts** for manufactured home transport. The only identified exception in the San Antonio TxDMV district is certain routes in Bandera County (SH 16 between SH 173 and Enchanted River Dr for loads over 17 feet high), which require a Bandera County Sheriff escort. Private pilot car operators handle virtually all escort duties.

Market rates for private pilot/escort cars (Heavy Haulers, HomeGuide, Angi data from 2025–2026): **$1.50–$2.00/mile** for standard lead or chase; **$2.25–$2.50/mile** for high-pole vehicles; **$400–$500/day** minimum for short runs.

## San Antonio's oversize-load route restrictions are uniquely challenging

The San Antonio TxDMV district maintains some of the most restrictive oversize-load regulations of any Texas metro, creating route-planning challenges that directly affect transport timing and cost. These restrictions are documented in the TxDMV San Antonio District Permit Restrictions file (updated on a rolling basis, with entries as recent as January 2026).

**The critical rule: no oversize transit through Loop 410.** Restriction #19802 (permanent, dated November 29, 2023) states: *"No width, no height, and/or 65' max length on all highways inside IH 410 unless the vehicle has an origin or destination here."* Any manufactured home exceeding the legal width of 8'6" — which is virtually all of them — cannot transit through the Loop 410 perimeter. Homes must have an origin or destination within the loop, or they must route around it entirely. A limited detour exception exists for loads up to 12 feet wide, 15 feet high, and 80 feet long via IH 10W → E.US 87S → E.IH 410.

**I-35 through San Antonio is severely restricted.** I-35 northbound between IH 10 and Rittiman Rd has a permanent **12-foot maximum width** restriction. The IH 10/IH 35/US 87 northbound to IH 10/US 87 westbound interchange carries a blanket **"NO PERMITS"** designation (as of August 2025). The massive **I-35 NEX construction program** — a $1.5 billion+ expansion project running from N. Walters Street to FM 1103 (~20 miles through NE San Antonio) — imposes frequent temporary closures through at least fall 2027. The I-35 NEX Central section (I-410 North to FM 3009 in Schertz) is over halfway complete; the South Phase 1 started in summer 2024 and targets late 2028 completion.

**Loop 1604 interchanges carry tight dimensional limits.** The junction of IH 10/US 87 and N. LP 1604 imposes permanent **12-foot max width and/or 80-foot max length** restrictions across multiple ramp movements. Some connections carry "NO PERMITS" designations (updated April 2025). Loop 1604 frontage roads near IH 10 have a **10-foot max width** restriction, and the N. LP 1604 westbound collector/distributor road is limited to **11-foot max width** near Leon Creek.

**IH-10 has time-restricted segments.** Between Camp Bullis Rd and De Zavala Rd, oversize loads face an **11-foot max width during 5:00 AM–9:00 PM Monday through Friday**, with no permits at all during other times and on weekends (dated May 2025). The IH 10 westbound exit ramp to La Cantera Pkwy has a permanent 12-foot width and 16-foot height limit.

**US-281 access is limited.** No permits are issued between US 181 and IH 410 (permanent restriction). The IH 37/US 281 northbound exit ramp to Marbach Rd also carries a permanent no-permits designation.

**Time-of-day restrictions** apply universally: oversize movement is limited to **30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset**, including weekends. Night travel is only permitted for loads ≤10 feet wide, ≤14 feet high, and ≤100 feet long on the Interstate system. Movement is prohibited during hazardous weather conditions. Transporters should contact the San Antonio TxDMV District at SAT.OSOW.Permits@TxDOT.gov or (210) 615-5912 for current construction zone updates.

## Interstate permit stacking adds modest but real costs

When manufactured homes cross state lines from San Antonio, each state requires its own separate permit. **No interstate reciprocity agreements exist** for oversize/overweight permits between Texas and its neighbors. The permit fees themselves are relatively modest, but escort requirement differences between states can significantly alter total costs.

### Texas + Louisiana (SA → Shreveport, Baton Rouge, New Orleans)

| Fee component | Single-wide | Double-wide (2 sections) |
|---|---|---|
| TxDMV MH permit ($40/section) | $40 | $80 |
| LaDOTD mobile home oversize permit ($10/trip, 72-hr validity) | $10 | $20 |
| LA escort vehicle permit (out-of-state company) | $10 | $10 |
| **Total state permit fees** | **~$60** | **~$110** |

Louisiana's permit structure is notably inexpensive at **$10 per trip** for mobile home oversize permits (LaDOTD, Act 301 of 2019). However, Louisiana requires **State Police escorts for any load exceeding 16 feet wide** on any highway — more restrictive than Texas's private-escort-only system. Louisiana also restricts loads wider than 12 feet from Interstates through Shreveport, Monroe, Lake Charles, Baton Rouge, and New Orleans during rush hours (7–9 AM, 3:30–5 PM M–F). Private escort operators in Louisiana must carry **$500,000 in general liability, motor vehicle liability, and workers' comp coverage** (La. R.S. 32:387.1). Mobile homes over 14 feet wide can travel Louisiana Interstates — an exception to the general rule prohibiting >14-foot loads on Interstate highways.

### Texas + New Mexico (SA → Albuquerque, Las Cruces)

| Fee component | Single-wide | Double-wide (2 sections) |
|---|---|---|
| TxDMV MH permit ($40/section) | $40 | $80 |
| NMDOT single-trip oversize permit | $25 | $50 |
| NM transaction fee + 5% service charge | ~$14 | ~$15 |
| **Total state permit fees** | **~$79** | **~$145** |

New Mexico charges a **$25 single-trip oversize permit** plus a **$12 transaction fee and 5% service charge** on all permits. Self-issue permit books are available at **$250 for 10 permits** ($25 each). A critical additional cost: NM State Police escorts, when required, cost **$300/day**. Loads up to 18 feet wide on designated 4-lane highways may not require escorts, but 2-lane highways require front and rear escorts for loads over 18 feet. If the move originates within New Mexico, a **property tax clearance certificate** is required from the county assessor. All commercial vehicles must stop at operating NM ports of entry.

### Texas + Oklahoma (SA → Oklahoma City, Tulsa)

| Fee component | Single-wide | Double-wide (2 sections) |
|---|---|---|
| TxDMV MH permit ($40/section) | $40 | $80 |
| ODOT manufactured home permit | $40 | $80 |
| OK processing/credit card fees | ~$4 | ~$5 |
| **Total state permit fees** | **~$84** | **~$165** |

Oklahoma's **$40 manufactured home single-trip permit** mirrors Texas's pricing. Oklahoma's escort thresholds are more restrictive: on 2-lane highways, **one front escort is required at widths exceeding 12 feet**, and two escorts (front and rear) are required above 14 feet. On multi-lane highways, one rear escort is needed above 14 feet. Oklahoma restricts oversize loads during rush hours in Cleveland, Oklahoma, and Tulsa counties (7–9 AM, 3:30–6:30 PM M–F). The ODOT annual envelope permit ($4,000) is explicitly **void for manufactured home use**.

### Multi-state permit cost summary

Total government permit fees for interstate moves from San Antonio are remarkably low — typically **$60–$170 for a double-wide** depending on destination state. The real cost escalation comes from **differing escort requirements** across state lines. A double-wide that requires no escorts in Texas may need State Police escorts in Louisiana or multiple private escorts in Oklahoma, adding hundreds to thousands of dollars to the move.

## Fuel surcharges and San Antonio's diesel price advantage

San Antonio's proximity to Gulf Coast refineries delivers a consistent fuel cost advantage for manufactured home transporters. As of March 2026, **San Antonio diesel prices average approximately $4.52/gallon** (AAA/Stacker data, March 16, 2026), compared to the **PADD 3 (Gulf Coast) average of $4.84** and the **national average of $5.07** (EIA Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update, released March 17, 2026). San Antonio runs roughly **$0.55/gallon below the national average** — a structural advantage driven by refinery proximity, lower Texas fuel taxes, and conventional (non-reformulated) fuel blends.

A manufactured home toter truck towing a loaded home typically achieves **4–6 MPG** (estimated based on Class 7–8 truck fuel consumption data from Geotab, OOIDA, and industry sources; the extreme wind drag from a 14–16 foot wide, 50–72 foot long structure significantly reduces fuel economy versus standard freight). At 5 MPG, San Antonio's $0.55/gallon diesel advantage translates to roughly **$0.11 per mile in fuel savings** versus the national average. On a 500-mile move, that's approximately $55 in savings per truck — meaningful but modest relative to total move costs.

| Diesel price (March 2026) | Fuel cost/mile at 5 MPG | vs. national average |
|---|---|---|
| **San Antonio: $4.52** | $0.90/mile | Saves $0.11/mile |
| Texas average: $4.61 | $0.92/mile | Saves $0.09/mile |
| PADD 3 Gulf Coast: $4.84 | $0.97/mile | Saves $0.05/mile |
| National average: $5.07 | $1.01/mile | Baseline |
| West Coast: $5.86 | $1.17/mile | Costs $0.16 more |
| California: $6.43 | $1.29/mile | Costs $0.27 more |

Most Texas manufactured home transporters **incorporate fuel costs into their all-in per-mile quotes** rather than breaking out a separate fuel surcharge line item. There is no industry-standard separate fuel surcharge specific to manufactured home transport. When surcharges are calculated, the standard trucking formula applies: **(Current EIA Diesel − Baseline Price) ÷ Truck MPG = surcharge per mile**. Using the DAT standard $1.25 baseline and San Antonio diesel of $4.52: ($4.52 − $1.25) ÷ 5 MPG = **$0.65/mile implied surcharge**, versus $0.76/mile at national average diesel.

Fuel represents an estimated **15–25% of transport-only cost** and **5–15% of full-service (all-in) cost**, since labor, setup, and equipment dominate total pricing. The March 2026 diesel prices reflect a sharp spike from early 2026 levels (PADD 3 was ~$3.16 in January 2026, per FRED/EIA) — an increase of approximately 53% in two months that transporters are actively passing through to customers.

## Texas Triangle corridors create 15–25% pricing discounts

The three major transport corridors connecting San Antonio to Houston, Dallas–Fort Worth, and Austin — the "Texas Triangle" — benefit from concentrated transporter competition and high move volume that pushes per-mile rates **an estimated 15–25% below comparable rural or low-traffic routes**.

**SA → Austin (I-35, ~80 miles)** is the most competitive corridor. The Cavco Industries factory in **Seguin** (35 miles east of SA on I-10) ships the lion's share of its production into the SA–Austin corridor, creating predictable, recurring transport demand. Multiple dealer clusters in New Braunfels (Titan Factory Direct), Seguin (Texas Built Mobile Homes, Factory Expo), and San Antonio (Alamo Homes, Clayton Homes, Manufactured Housing Consultants) generate constant short-haul traffic. Single-wide transport-only costs on this corridor run **$2,500–$5,000**, with full-service at **$5,000–$8,000** — at the low end of the 50–100 mile tier due to competition.

**SA → Houston (I-10, ~200 miles)** connects Texas's 2nd and 4th largest metros with heavy manufactured home activity in both regions. Multiple companies serve this route: Martinez Mobile Homes, Texas Elite MHS, Texas Mobile Home Transport, and national brokers. Houston-area average new double-wide prices were $115,400 in Q1 2025. Single-wide full-service costs run **$7,000–$14,000** for this corridor.

**SA → DFW (I-35, ~275 miles)** benefits from factory-to-dealer flows: Champion Home Builders operates plants in Burleson and Mansfield (both near DFW), while Seguin-area factories ship northbound. The corridor passes through Austin and Waco, where intermediate pickups can reduce deadhead costs. Single-wide full-service costs run **$8,000–$15,000**.

**Rural and remote route premiums** offset the Triangle advantage. Moves to West Texas (Permian Basin), the Rio Grande Valley, or the Panhandle carry **20–40% higher per-mile costs** due to longer deadhead distances, fewer competing transporters, and more complex routing. The fundamental problem in manufactured home transport is that **every return trip is deadhead** — the specialized toter cannot carry general cargo back. On Triangle corridors, transporters mitigate this by scheduling back-to-back jobs, running multi-stop circuits, and maintaining volume contracts with dealers.

The current market conditions amplify competitive pricing. Per Mobile ByeBye's 2025 market analysis and TMHA survey data: **14,700 unsold units sit on Texas dealer lots** (nearly double the 7,900 in mid-2022), factory lead times have dropped to 6 weeks, and **71% of retailers have cut prices at least twice since January 2024**. Many dealers now offer **factory-paid delivery within 150–250 miles** as a competitive incentive, effectively absorbing $4,000–$8,000+ in transport costs into home pricing. Transportation crews report available capacity, giving buyers leverage in negotiating transport rates.

## Conclusion: what these numbers mean for pricing decisions

Three structural factors make San Antonio one of the most cost-effective origins (or destinations) for manufactured home transport in the country. First, the **$40 flat permit fee with zero distance escalation** means government costs are negligible at any distance — a stark contrast to states with mileage-based or county-based permit structures. Second, **escort requirements are permissive**: most standard single-wide and properly-sectioned double-wide homes (≤16 ft per section) require no escorts at all in Texas, eliminating what would otherwise be a $1.50–$3.50/mile addition. Third, the **Texas Triangle corridor competition and current buyer's-market glut** create favorable negotiating conditions for consumers.

The most important route-planning takeaway is the **Loop 410 transit prohibition** — any move crossing San Antonio without an origin or destination inside the loop must route around the entire metro perimeter, potentially adding 20–40 miles and requiring careful coordination with TxDMV's San Antonio district. I-35 NEX construction through 2027–2028 further constrains northbound oversize routing.

For interstate moves, the permit stacking costs are modest ($60–$170 total for a double-wide), but differing state escort requirements — particularly Louisiana's State Police escort mandate for loads over 16 feet — can materially increase total costs. Fuel savings from San Antonio's low diesel prices ($0.11/mile versus national average) provide a small but consistent per-mile advantage that compounds over long distances.

These figures should be treated as market ranges rather than firm quotes; actual pricing varies with home condition, age, season, specific route, and transporter availability. All data is current as of early 2026, sourced from TxDMV fee schedules (May 2025 Motor Carrier Handbook), EIA diesel data (March 17, 2026), TMHA market reports (February 2026), and multiple transport company and cost-platform sources dated 2024–2026.

## FEMA deployments and severe weather commandeer transporters at unpredictable intervals

San Antonio occupies one of the most weather-exposed positions in the manufactured home transport market. The metro sits in "Flash Flood Alley," borders Tornado Alley, and serves as a primary federal disaster-staging corridor for Gulf Coast hurricanes. Severe weather affects transport pricing through two channels: **direct damage requiring emergency relocations** and **transporter diversion to FEMA contracts**.

**Hurricane Harvey (August 2017)** remains the benchmark disruption. FEMA projected **8,500 manufactured housing units** for deployment to Texas survivors — later determined to be an overestimate by ~2,600 units, costing **$152.4 million in unnecessary procurement, transport, and storage** (DHS OIG Report OIG-20-15, February 2020). The Beeville staging area, a former naval base **90 miles southeast of San Antonio** and one of only three national FEMA disaster staging areas, stored approximately **2,400 single- and double-wide homes plus 200 additional trailers/RVs**. Transporting thousands of MHUs from factories and national storage sites (Cumberland, MD; Selma, AL; Sherwood, LA) to Texas, then distributing them to individual survivor sites, absorbed enormous manufactured home transport capacity. DAT Solutions reported **spot-market truckload rates jumped 6 percentage points** post-Harvey; fuel costs spiked **15% nationally** because Texas refineries (representing ~30% of U.S. capacity) temporarily shut down.

**Hurricane Beryl (July 2024)** followed a different pattern. Making landfall as a Category 1 between Matagorda and Galveston on July 8, 2024, Beryl generated FEMA disaster declaration DR-4798 covering **121 affected counties** with **$750+ million** approved for nearly 700,000 households. However, FEMA relied primarily on Transitional Sheltering Assistance (hotel-based) rather than large-scale MHU deployment, reducing direct transporter diversion. Gulf Coast manufactured home values still dropped **18% below 2023 levels** from insurance premium spikes.

**San Antonio's hail and tornado exposure** is extreme in its own right. The metro area recorded **222 ground-level hail reports** from trained spotters in a recent 12-month period and **79 severe weather warnings**. Texas sustained **$6.5 billion** in hail property damage from 2012–2021 (highest nationally). The **April 28, 2021 hailstorm** caused **$3.7 billion in damages** across central Texas. In May 2025, a confirmed tornado near I-10 in Bexar County damaged homes and businesses across the metro. NOAA data shows manufactured home residents are **15–20 times more likely to die in tornadoes** than occupants of permanent structures, and EF-1 winds can completely destroy a manufactured home — generating urgent emergency transport demand when homes must be relocated or replaced.

The **June 2025 San Antonio floods** (record **3.98 inches/hour** rainfall; 13 killed; joint disaster declaration) and the catastrophic **July 4, 2025 Hill Country floods** (Guadalupe River rose 26 feet in 45 minutes; **135+ killed** in Kerr County) added a flooding dimension to the severe-weather transport disruption profile. **Winter Storm Uri (February 2021)** demonstrated cold-weather vulnerability: all 254 Texas counties were affected, road closures shut down transport statewide, and 500,000 insurance claims were filed — though burst pipes rather than structural destruction meant limited MHU deployment.

## The JBSA military PCS cycle concentrates demand into a 16-week window

Joint Base San Antonio is the **largest concentration of military installations in the Department of Defense**, with **67,350 direct full-time employees** including **32,333 active-duty personnel** across Lackland AFB, Randolph AFB, Fort Sam Houston, and Camp Bullis (Texas Comptroller, 2023). The base generates an estimated **$55.1 billion** in total economic output and supports a total workforce impact of **240,403 jobs** including indirect employment.

During peak PCS season (May through August), JBSA processes approximately **4,500 household goods shipments** — roughly 60–65% of annual PCS volume compressed into 16 weeks. With service members rotating every 2–3 years, JBSA generates an estimated **10,000–16,000 PCS-related moves annually** (inbound and outbound combined). The manufactured home component is small but structurally significant: DoD regulations (USTRANSCOM DTR Part IV, Attachment K5) allow service members to ship a manufactured home **in lieu of household goods**, though the regulation itself warns that "experience shows that few mobile homes are moved without exceeding prescribed entitlements." Given that approximately **6–7% of Texas housing stock is manufactured** and military families skew toward budget-constrained junior enlisted personnel, an estimated **1–3% of JBSA PCS moves** may involve manufactured home transport — translating to roughly **100–480 manufactured home moves annually** directly attributable to military PCS.

The indirect effect is larger. Multiple manufactured home communities cluster near JBSA installations — **Lackland Mobile Home & RV Park**, **Military Highway Mobile Home Park** (bordering Lackland's perimeter), **Ridge Crest MHC** (10–20 minutes from Lackland), and **Hidden Lake Mobile Home Park** (1.5 miles from Fort Sam Houston's Martindale Army Airfield). MHVillage lists **18+ manufactured home communities** near Fort Sam Houston alone. Military families cycling in and out of these communities every 2–3 years create steady manufactured home buying, selling, and repositioning demand throughout the PCS season — even when the home itself isn't transported cross-country.

A major policy shift looms: on **May 28, 2025, the Pentagon ordered military services to cut discretionary PCS budgets by 50% by FY2030** (10% by FY2027, 30% by FY2028, 40% by FY2029, 50% by FY2030). If implemented at JBSA proportionally, this could reduce annual PCS-driven transport demand by thousands of moves over the next four years.

## Population growth and housing affordability fuel structural transport demand

San Antonio added approximately **23,945 residents** in 2023–2024, ranking **fourth nationally** for numeric population growth. The city now claims roughly **1.57 million residents** (surpassing Philadelphia to become the **6th largest U.S. city** by early 2026), while the metro area approaches **2.8 million**. From April 2020 to July 2024, the metro gained nearly **205,000 people** — **59% from domestic migration** (~121,000 from other states, led by California, Florida, and New York relocations). Bexar County alone absorbed **49,946 in-migrants** from other states in 2022.

This growth feeds directly into manufactured home transport demand. Texas received **18,343 manufactured home shipments in 2024** — more than the next two states combined (Florida at 7,405 and North Carolina at 6,768). Every shipped home requires transport from factory to dealer, then dealer to homesite. Multi-section homes — now comprising over half of Texas shipments (722 of 1,312 in January 2026) — each require **multiple transport trips**. Proportionally, the San Antonio metro likely absorbs **1,600–1,800+ manufactured homes annually**, generating an estimated **3,000–4,000+ individual transport trips** when multi-section logistics are included.

The affordability gap drives this demand. San Antonio's **median site-built home** sells for approximately **$290,000–$310,000** (2025). A new manufactured home in Texas averages **$86,700 for a single-section** and **$146,900 for a double-section** (2024 Census Bureau MHS data) — representing **58–70% savings** before land costs. San Antonio already has the **most affordable housing among Texas's four major metros**, requiring just **$89,864** in household income to afford a median home versus $138,781 in Austin. This affordability positioning makes manufactured housing a natural entry point for the roughly 24,000 new residents arriving annually, many from high-cost markets.

However, the market is currently in cyclical contraction. TMHA forecasts **15,882 Texas shipments** for 2026 (down from 18,343 in 2024), with dealer inventory at **14,700 unsold units statewide** — nearly double the 7,900 in mid-2022. Factory-gate prices for new multi-sections fell from **$134,700 in 2023** to **$114,200 by Q1 2025** (15% nominal decline). Industry participants expect a mid-2026 rebound.

## Eagle Ford Shale makes San Antonio a workforce housing staging hub

San Antonio serves as the primary staging and supply hub for manufactured workforce housing deployed to Eagle Ford Shale oilfield areas. The Eagle Ford extends south and east of San Antonio across roughly 38 counties, with core drilling counties — **Webb, DeWitt, Karnes, La Salle, Dimmit** — all within **60–120 miles** of the city via I-35 and connecting highways.

Several San Antonio-based companies explicitly market oilfield workforce housing transport. **Mi Casa Housing** (San Antonio) builds, transports, and installs workforce units across the Eagle Ford, Permian Basin, and Bakken, maintaining its own trucks and translifts. **Manufactured Housing Consultants** (San Antonio) lists "Oil Field Housing" as a dedicated product category. **Texas Built Mobile Homes** (Seguin, 35 miles east of SA) specializes in scalable workforce configurations up to 10-bedroom dormitories for both Eagle Ford and Permian Basin operations.

Eagle Ford rig counts reveal the demand cycle's volatility: **~95 rigs pre-pandemic** (2019), crashing to **just 10 in August 2020**, recovering to **71 by March 2023**, then settling to **~55 by early 2024** as operators consolidated. The basin produced **1.147 million barrels/day** in March 2024, and ConocoPhillips' Marathon Oil acquisition positions the Eagle Ford as a "major growth driver." Critically, the Eagle Ford has the **fewest drilled-but-uncompleted (DUC) wells** of any major U.S. shale basin (347 as of March 2024), meaning future production requires new drilling and sustained workforce presence — not just completions of existing wells.

The Permian Basin (centered 300+ miles west around Midland/Odessa) has its own localized supply chain, though SA-based companies like Mi Casa Housing and Texas Built do serve it for manufactured housing wholesale and long-haul transport. San Antonio's role is more dominant for the Eagle Ford, where the geographic proximity makes it the natural logistics hub.

## Park closures and infrastructure displacement create forced-move demand spikes

A landmark **January 2020 UT Austin School of Law report** — "Endangered: San Antonio's Vanishing Mobile Home Parks and a Path for Preservation" — documented that **9 mobile home parks closed since 2014**, with **5 condemned in 2018 alone** through proactive city inspections. Approximately **88 active parks** remained, housing **~22,000 residents** in **~8,000 mobile homes** with a median household income of **$36,000**.

The defining displacement case was **Mission Trails Mobile Home Park** (1515 Mission Road), where City Council voted in 2014 to rezone the property for a $75-million luxury apartment development. **~300 residents** were displaced and **106 mobile homes** had to be relocated — the **largest residential displacement in San Antonio since Hemisfair Park in the 1960s**. A 2017 study by Vecinos de Mission Trails documented devastating consequences: **1 in 4 displaced households experienced homelessness**; nearly half reported new or worsened health problems; relocation payments ranged from just $5,600 to $7,500 per household.

The UT Law report notes that many older mobile homes "would break apart if moved," meaning park closures generate transport demand only for structurally sound newer units while destroying housing for the remainder. In a positive counter-example, **Riverside Terrace** residents formed a co-op in November 2023 and purchased their 46-unit park for **$3 million** with $3.9 million in city support, preserving it as Mission Trail Community Association.

The major infrastructure projects — **I-35 NEX** ($1.5 billion central phase, $852 million south phase) and **Loop 1604 North expansion** ($1.4 billion, 23 miles) — were engineered to minimize displacement. TxDOT chose elevated lanes fitting within existing right-of-way specifically because of "extensive development along frontage roads." **Port San Antonio** and **Brooks** redevelopments displaced former military housing (site-built), not manufactured home communities. The transport demand from displacement is therefore driven primarily by **code enforcement closures and private redevelopment** rather than infrastructure projects.

## Winter Texan snowbird migration barely registers in San Antonio's transport market

San Antonio is emphatically **not** a Winter Texan destination. The seasonal "snowbird" phenomenon is overwhelmingly concentrated in the **Rio Grande Valley** (McAllen, Mission, Harlingen, Brownsville), which hosts approximately **100,000 Winter Texans** (57,000 households per UTRGV's 2022–23 survey) across **200+ dedicated RV and manufactured home resort communities**. The RGV Winter Texan economy generates **$761 million in direct spending** and **$1.9 billion** in total economic impact annually.

San Antonio's manufactured home communities are **year-round permanent-resident affordable housing**, not seasonal resorts. No SA-area community was found that specifically markets to or accommodates Winter Texans. The Hill Country between San Antonio and Austin does attract some seasonal visitors, but they arrive in personal RVs — not transported manufactured homes. The seasonal arrival pattern (October–November) and departure (March–April) with peak occupancy January–March has **no measurable impact** on SA-area manufactured home transport demand.

Where San Antonio does rank highly is as a **permanent retirement destination** — **#2 nationally** for retiree in-migration (SmartAsset 2024) — driven by no state income tax, housing costs 21% below national averages, and "Military City USA" identity attracting ~250,000 veteran retirees. But these permanent relocations involve conventional home purchases, not seasonal manufactured home transport.

## TxDOT travel windows create de facto day-of-week pricing tiers

Texas Transportation Code Chapter 623, Subchapter E, and TxDMV administrative rules (43 TAC Chapter 219) impose travel restrictions that effectively tier scheduling costs by day and time, even though weekend moves are technically permitted.

**Daylight-only movement** is the binding constraint. Manufactured homes may travel only during **30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset**. This means a December move in San Antonio has roughly **10.3 usable hours** versus **14.25 hours in June** — a **38% reduction** in daily transport capacity. Winter moves may require additional travel days for the same distance, increasing per-move costs through crew time, overnight staging, and permit duration. Single-trip permits are valid for a maximum of **5 days** at **$40 per permit**.

**All manufactured housing transport is prohibited on six national holidays** (New Year's Day, Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, Christmas) — a stricter standard than general oversize loads, which restrict only loads exceeding certain dimensions. This removes **six high-demand scheduling days** from the calendar, compressing moves into adjacent periods and creating scheduling premiums around holiday weeks.

**Urban curfew windows** restrict travel through San Antonio's metro core, typically during **6:00–9:00 AM and 3:00–6:00 PM** rush hours, as specified on each individual permit. This effectively reduces usable urban transport hours by 6 hours daily, forcing either early-morning departures or midday-only urban transits.

**Weekend transport is permitted** during daylight hours with no blanket restriction. However, weekend moves carry a **practical premium** for several reasons: escort vehicle operators (required for homes over 16 feet wide at **$1.50–$3.50 per mile each**) charge overtime rates; TxDOT permits must be obtained in advance; and crew availability is constrained. Industry sources indicate weekend premiums of roughly **10–20%** are common though not universal. **Weather halts** — movement is prohibited when road surfaces are hazardous from rain, ice, wind, or other conditions — add unpredictable cost during spring storm season and winter freeze events.

The net effect: **midweek moves during off-peak months** (particularly Tuesday through Thursday in January or February) represent the lowest-cost scheduling window. **Weekend moves during peak summer** (especially weeks bracketing the July 4 blackout) represent the highest-cost window — combining peak seasonal demand, holiday compression, and overtime labor premiums.

## Conclusion

San Antonio's manufactured home transport market is shaped by an unusually dense concentration of demand drivers that stack, compound, and occasionally collide. **Six distinct forces** — PCS military cycling, population-growth-driven new placements, FEMA disaster deployments, Eagle Ford workforce housing, park displacement relocations, and the TxDOT regulatory calendar — each follow their own seasonal rhythm but converge most intensely from May through August. ## How to verify a San Antonio transporter before signing a contract

A San Antonio homeowner should complete four verification steps before hiring any manufactured home transport company. Each step takes 5–15 minutes online.

**Step 1 — Verify TDHCA installer license.** Navigate to the TDHCA license lookup tool at **https://mhweb.tdhca.state.tx.us/mhweb/license_view.jsp** (last updated February 27, 2026). Search by company name or license number. Texas does not issue a separate "transporter" license — the **Installer license** covers both transport and installation. Confirm the license status shows "Active" and note the expiration date. All installers must carry a **$25,000 surety bond**. If the company holds a Retailer/Installer (R/I) license, the bond is $50,000.

**Step 2 — Verify FMCSA operating authority and safety record.** Go to the FMCSA SAFER Company Snapshot at **https://safer.fmcsa.dot.gov/CompanySnapshot.aspx**. Search by USDOT number (ask the company for this number — a legitimate operator will provide it immediately). Confirm the carrier's status shows "ACTIVE" with no out-of-service orders. Check the 24-month inspection and crash history. For **interstate moves only**, verify that the company holds active operating authority (MC number) and has filed proof of **$750,000 minimum public liability insurance** via FMCSA's Licensing & Insurance page at **https://li-public.fmcsa.dot.gov**. Note: most San Antonio-area operators are intrastate-only and will show "NOT AUTHORIZED" for interstate authority — this is normal if they only operate within Texas.

**Step 3 — Verify TxDMV oversize-load permit authority.** The company must obtain a **TxDMV single-trip manufactured housing permit** ($40 per trip; each half of a double-wide requires a separate permit) through the TxPROS system at **https://txpros.txdmv.gov**. Ask to see the permit before the scheduled move date. The permit specifies the approved route, curfew times, and required escort configuration:

- Loads **≤16 feet wide**: no escort required
- Loads **>16 to ≤18 feet wide**: one escort (front on two-lane roads, rear on four-lane)
- Loads **>18 feet wide**: two escorts (front and rear)

Transport is restricted to **daylight hours only** (half-hour before sunrise to half-hour after sunset) and prohibited on all six federal holidays.

**Step 4 — Verify insurance coverage.** Request a current certificate of insurance (COI) showing:

- **Commercial auto/trucking liability**: minimum $500,000 for intrastate carriers (Texas Transportation Code Ch. 643); $750,000 for interstate
- **Cargo insurance**: FMCSA minimums are $5,000 per vehicle / $10,000 per occurrence, but industry practice for manufactured home transport should be **$100,000+ cargo coverage** to protect the home's value in transit
- **Active surety bond**: $25,000 (installer) or $50,000 (retailer/installer) filed with TDHCA

**Red flags to watch for:** Any company that cannot provide a USDOT number, refuses to show a TDHCA license number, quotes suspiciously low prices (under $2,000 for a full-service local move), requests full payment upfront, or has a BBB rating of F. One San Antonio operator — **Lone Star Mobile Home & Residential** (BBB rating: F, owner Nathan Supinger) — has two unanswered BBB complaints and two licensing alerts for failure to maintain required competency licensing. Consumers should avoid this company.

**Where to file complaints:** TDHCA Manufactured Housing Division at **(877) 313-3023** or installations@tdhca.state.tx.us for licensing and installation issues; TxDMV at **(800) 299-1700** for transport permit violations; FMCSA at **1-888-DOT-SAFT** for interstate safety concerns.

## 1. Wind zone compliance is simpler than expected — but a trap exists

**Bexar County is classified as HUD Wind Zone I, not Wind Zone II.** The Texas Manufactured Housing Standards Act and TDHCA Rules (Title 10 TAC Chapter 80, effective March 3, 2024) designate only 15 coastal counties as Wind Zone II — Aransas, Brazoria, Calhoun, Cameron, Chambers, Galveston, Jefferson, Kenedy, Kleberg, Matagorda, Nueces, Orange, Refugio, San Patricio, and Willacy. Every other Texas county, including Bexar, falls under Wind Zone I.

The hidden cost here is **directional**: a Wind Zone I home purchased in San Antonio **cannot legally be installed in a Wind Zone II coastal county**. This rule applies to any home built on or after September 1, 1997. Conversely, a Wind Zone II home can always be installed in Zone I. Standard Wind Zone I tie-down installation runs **$1,000–$2,500** for a single-wide and **$1,500–$3,500** for a double-wide (HomeGuide, 2026; CashNowForMyHome, 2025). Buyers moving homes within the San Antonio metro should budget for this increase.

## 4. CPS Energy and SAWS fees are modest individually but add up fast

**CPS Energy** — San Antonio's sole electric and gas provider — publishes a detailed fee schedule (Policy for Miscellaneous Customer Charges, effective February 1, 2016, still current). The critical distinction for manufactured home moves is **where** the disconnect/reconnect occurs.

Reconnection at the meter costs **$24 electric / $36 gas / $53 combined** during regular hours, rising to **$30 / $47 / $69** during premium (after-hours/weekend) hours. But manufactured home removal typically requires disconnection at the **pole or gas main** — not merely the meter. Reconnection at the pole or underground transformer costs **$60 regular / $120 premium** for electric. Gas reconnection at the main costs **$400 regular / $600 premium** — by far the largest single CPS fee. Temporary electric service for the construction/setup period adds **$150–$225**, and rescheduled connections cost **$58 each**. New service line extensions take approximately **40 days** and are priced per-project based on CPS Energy's Customer Construction Cost methodology, updated through a 5-phase adjustment completed July 1, 2024.

**SAWS** (San Antonio Water System) fees are lighter: meter trip fees of **$12** per visit, same-day rush service at **$35**, and new-account deposits of **$60–$100** depending on services (SAWS Special Services Fees, approved January 2, 2026).

For **rural placements without municipal sewer**, the OSSF (On-Site Sewage Facility) permit through Bexar County costs approximately **$230** (Texas Super Septic, current pricing). The site evaluation runs **$700–$1,500**, and system installation ranges from **$6,300–$10,000** for conventional septic to **$7,800–$9,800** for aerobic spray systems to **$9,800–$20,000** for aerobic drip systems (Texas Septic Guide, 2026; AJ Septic Services, 2025). San Antonio's expansive clay soil drains too slowly for conventional systems, making **aerobic systems the default** — roughly double the cost. State-mandated aerobic maintenance contracts add **$500–$700 every two years** (TCEQ requirement).

## 5. San Antonio's split geology demands site-specific foundation engineering

Bexar County straddles two dramatically different geological zones, and the **soil transition line running roughly along I-35** means adjacent lots can require entirely different — and differently expensive — foundation approaches.

**East and south San Antonio** sits on the Blackland Prairie, dominated by **Houston Black clay (vertisol)** that swells when wet and shrinks during drought, exerting up to **14,000 psi** on foundations. This expansive clay causes differential settlement that destroys standard pier-and-beam installations. Geotechnical soil tests cost **$300–$1,000** for basic residential analysis and **$1,000–$5,000** for full borings (HomeGuide, 2026; Geotech Engineering & Testing). A standard pier-and-beam foundation runs **$1,000–$2,000** but performs poorly on this clay. Engineered slab foundations — typically post-tensioned — cost **$4,000–$10,000**, while crawl-space foundations run **$6,000–$15,000** (Manufactured Housing Consultants, 2024–2025). Foundation repair after improper initial placement averages **$5,000–$7,000** in San Antonio, with major repairs reaching **$15,000–$30,000** (G.L. Hunt Foundation Repair; Two Bros Foundation Repair, 2025).

**North and northwest San Antonio** (Helotes, Stone Oak, Bulverde, Fair Oaks Ranch) transitions to **Edwards Plateau limestone and caliche**. Standard ground anchors cannot penetrate this substrate. TDHCA Rules §80.55(d)(4) require **cross-drive rock anchors** when auger anchors fail, with rods fully inserted to a minimum **24 inches embedded** depth. Helical pier foundations for rock substrate cost **$1,500–$4,000 per pier** installed, with 5–10 piers typical for a manufactured home — totaling **$7,500–$40,000** (Today's Homeowner, 2026; HomeGuide, 2026). Rock excavation requires saws and hydraulic hammers, adding significant surcharges over standard trenching.

## 6. Flood zone elevation can cost more than the manufactured home itself

San Antonio's flood-prone corridors — the San Antonio River, Salado Creek, Leon Creek, Olmos Creek, and Medina River — place thousands of manufactured home sites within FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas. San Antonio's Unified Development Code Appendix F requires manufactured homes in AE, AH, and A1-30 zones to have their **lowest floor elevated to BFE + 1 foot** (one foot above Base Flood Elevation). New homes placed outside existing parks, or in new or expanded parks, must meet this full BFE+1 standard.

An Elevation Certificate prepared by a licensed surveyor costs **$350–$500** (EHM of Texas, 2026). The real expense is the elevated foundation: fill-method elevation systems cost **$5,000–$15,000+** depending on required height, while engineered elevated pier/pile foundations for homes needing 3+ feet of elevation run **$10,000–$40,000+** (FEMA P-85; Manufactured Housing Consultants, 2024–2025; Dawson Foundation Repair). Full manufactured home lifting services cost **$10–$18 per square foot** (Dawson Foundation Repair, 2024–2025), meaning a 1,500-square-foot double-wide could cost **$15,000–$27,000** to lift alone.

NFIP flood insurance for manufactured homes averages approximately **$750/year** but ranges from **$300–$3,000+** annually depending on elevation relative to BFE (Policygenius, 2023; FEMA FloodSmart, 2025). A Floodplain Development Permit from Bexar County costs **$50 per activity**, and engineering flood studies required by the city or county run **$3,000–$10,000+**.

The City of San Antonio and Bexar County are in a **multi-phase flood map modernization process**. As of March 2023, "Best Available Models" have been submitted to FEMA Region 6 for Phase 3 processing (City of San Antonio Information Bulletin IB 582, March 2023). Updated maps may reclassify areas — properties not previously in an SFHA may be added, triggering new elevation and insurance requirements for the first time.

## 8. Pre-1976 homes face a market that mostly refuses to move them

Homes built before June 15, 1976 predate the federal HUD Code and carry structural, regulatory, and insurance liabilities that make **an estimated 50–75% of Texas transporters refuse the job entirely** (ConsumerAffairs, 2026; HomeNation; SmartFinancial, 2024). Robert Spencer, owner of K&E Transport, noted to ConsumerAffairs (2026): "Although we do transport these older models, we can't legally set them down anywhere if they haven't been updated through the HUD renovation process."

Required structural reinforcement before transport includes frame reinforcement, floor joist repair, and roof truss strengthening at **$2,000–$5,000**. Full HUD renovation/compliance upgrades — structural, electrical, and plumbing — cost **$3,000–$10,000+**. Pre-move structural inspection runs **$200–$500**. Transport companies that accept pre-1976 homes charge a **30–50% premium** over standard rates due to liability exposure. Axle and tire replacement for homes that have been stationary for decades adds **$1,000–$3,000**, and metal strapping/over-roof tie-down retrofits cost **$1,500–$4,000** (multiple industry sources, 2024–2026).

Insurance compounds the problem: Progressive notes it "may be difficult to insure a mobile home built prior to 1976," with premiums **$500–$1,500 higher** than post-HUD homes when coverage is available at all. One favorable TDHCA rule: homes built before September 1, 1997 may be installed in either Wind Zone I or II counties without wind zone restrictions (TDHCA Rules Chapter 80, effective March 3, 2024).

## 9. Transit insurance leaves most homes fully exposed during the move

Standard manufactured home insurance (HO-7 policies) **explicitly excludes in-transit damage**. Progressive states: "Typically, mobile home insurance policies don't provide coverage while the home is being transported." Insurify (2026) confirms: "Damage done while moving: Mobile home insurance policies don't cover the home while it's in transit."

The available patches are thin. **Trip Collision Coverage** — an endorsement on an existing policy offering 30-day coverage — costs approximately **$75–$300** but covers only collision damage, not hail, wind, or road debris. A **standalone transportation endorsement** costs **$200–$500** per move. The transporter's Motor Truck Cargo insurance is typically included in the transport quote but may carry significant coverage limits and exclusions — multiple sources warn that "the transporter's insurance might have limitations that don't fully align with your needs" (Insuranceopedia).

San Antonio-specific risk amplifies the gap: Texas experiences more hail damage than any other state, with hail accounting for **30% of annual insurance claims** and average residential hail damage claims running approximately **$12,000** (Mint Exteriors/Restore Masters). Texas averages **132 tornadoes annually**, concentrated April through June. During transport, a manufactured home is fully exposed on a highway flatbed with no shelter from any of these perils. The Consumer Federation of America estimates **35% of manufactured homeowners nationwide carry no insurance at all**, meaning many homes in transit have zero coverage.

## 10. TCEQ asbestos and OSSF rules create a two-front compliance cost

**Asbestos** in pre-1980 manufactured homes is regulated by the Texas Department of State Health Services (DSHS) under the Texas Asbestos Health Protection Rules (25 TAC §295.31–.73) and federally under NESHAP (40 CFR Part 61, Subpart M). A critical distinction: **single-family mobile homes are exempt from NESHAP notification requirements** — but mobile home parks where the owner holds more than 4 units **are** subject to NESHAP. Regardless of the exemption, asbestos exposure during transport creates real liability.

Asbestos inspection costs **$250–$500** for a manufactured home, with laboratory analysis at **$25–$50 per sample** via PLM method (industry estimates; Mold Inspection Sciences of Austin). Pre-1980 manufactured homes commonly contain asbestos in floor tiles, siding, insulation, pipe wrap, and ceiling tiles. If asbestos-containing materials are found, abatement costs **$1,000–$3,000** for typical residential projects, scaling to **$5,000–$7,700** for extensive contamination, at a rate of **$5–$21 per square foot** (ACompanyThatBuysHouses, 2024–2025; Angi, 2024–2025). DSHS notification fees, when NESHAP applies, start at **$55 plus a 3% subscription fee** (TAHPR §295.61(j)).

**OSSF permits** for rural placements (covered in detail in Category 4 above) represent TCEQ's other major cost vector. The Bexar County permit costs approximately **$230**, while surrounding counties charge more — **$375 in Blanco County** and **$610 in Hays County** (Texas Super Septic, current pricing). Properties on the Edwards Aquifer Recharge Zone often require **aerobic drip systems** as the only permissible option, pushing total OSSF costs to **$15,000–$20,000+**.

## 11. Power line lifts and heritage trees are the route-planning minefield

CPS Energy does not publish a standard per-crossing fee for temporary power line lifts; requests go through the Customer Design & Delivery department (210-353-4050) and are assessed per-project. Industry-standard costs run **$100–$300 per crossing** for simple lifts and **$200–$500 per crossing** when lines must be de-energized and physically raised (transport industry sources, 2024–2025). If pole relocation is needed — rare but possible on constrained routes — costs jump to **$2,000–$8,000** for a simple wood pole and **$20,000–$75,000+** for complex relocations involving transformers (Quora, May 2024). On a typical San Antonio-area transport route of 10–30 miles, a manufactured home encounters **5–15 overhead power line crossings**, creating a total line-lift cost of **$500–$7,500** depending on route and home height.

San Antonio's **heritage tree ordinance** (UDC §35-523) protects any tree **24 inches or greater DBH** (diameter at breast height), or **12 inches DBH** for designated small species like Texas mountain laurel and Texas redbud. Heritage trees require **100% preservation** — removal without mitigation triggers civil penalties. Tree permit fees run **$50 per lot plus $100 review** for residential properties (UDC Appendix C, §35-C110). When heritage trees must be removed, mitigation replanting uses a ratio greater than 1:1, with replacement trees at minimum **1.5-inch caliper** planted in pervious areas of at least **162 square feet per tree**. A heritage live oak removal with mitigation can exceed **$2,500 per tree** including crane rental and replanting (CountBricks, 2025). Standard protected tree removal in San Antonio runs **$1,020–$2,240** with crane assist.

An additional constraint: San Antonio's **oak wilt ordinance** (Chapter 21, Section 170) requires all cuts or wounds on oak trees to be sealed within 30 minutes, with pruning minimized from **February 1 through July 1** — precisely overlapping with the spring moving season.

## 12. Edwards Aquifer compliance adds $10,000–$30,000+ to northern placements

The Edwards Aquifer Recharge Zone runs through San Antonio's fastest-growing suburban corridor — Helotes, Leon Springs, Stone Oak, the 1604/281 interchange area — and extends into Comal and Hays counties. TCEQ regulates all construction activity on the Recharge Zone, Transition Zone, and Contributing Zone under **30 TAC Chapter 213**.

Any construction-related activity on the Recharge Zone requires a **Water Pollution Abatement Plan (WPAP)** approved by TCEQ before work begins. The TCEQ application fee alone is **$650 for a single residential home on less than 5 acres** (TCEQ Form TCEQ-0574, revised February 24, 2015). But the application fee is the smallest piece: WPAP preparation by an engineer or environmental consultant costs **$5,000–$15,000+**, requiring PE-stamped plans, stormwater calculations, BMP design, and a Stormwater Pollution Prevention Plan. A required **geologic assessment** by a licensed geologist — including field mapping, stratigraphic analysis, and karst feature identification — adds **$2,000–$5,000+** (TCEQ Form TCEQ-0585 requirements). Geologic assessments expire **10 years after certification** (effective November 1, 2018).

Permanent stormwater Best Management Practices (BMPs) must achieve **80% Total Suspended Solids removal**, which may require rain gardens, retention ponds, or pervious pavement at a cost of **$5,000–$25,000+** for design and construction. SAWS requires a **Category Status Letter** to determine impervious cover limits within its service area — limits range from **15% to unrestricted** depending on location. Bexar County's Post-Construction Permit adds **$50 application + $200 review** for projects disturbing one acre or more, with **quarterly maintenance reporting** as an ongoing obligation.

Contributing Zone projects disturbing **5 or more acres** require a Contributing Zone Plan (CZP) with fees starting at **$1,500** for projects under 5 acres and scaling to **$4,000** for 10–40 acres (TCEQ-0574 fee schedule). The total compliance cost for placing a single manufactured home on the Edwards Aquifer Recharge Zone — TCEQ fees, engineering, geologic assessment, and BMP installation — reaches **$10,000–$30,000+**, a cost that simply does not exist for an identical placement outside the Recharge Zone boundary.

HomeAdvisor/Angi places the national full-service average at **$18,500** with a range of $12,000–$25,000; CostHelper quotes transport-only at $2,600–$3,500 and full-service at $10,000–$14,000 (likely conservative). The three-section "marriage" process is more complex than a double-wide, requiring precision alignment across two seam lines and substantially more engineering time. Triple-wides weighing **90,000–100,000 lbs** may trigger TxDMV overweight permits ($90 base fee plus county-specific charges) in addition to standard manufactured housing permits.

## Part B — Frequently asked questions

### 1. How much does it cost to move a single-wide mobile home in San Antonio?

A full-service single-wide move within 50 miles of San Antonio typically costs **$4,000–$6,000**, with the market median near **$5,000**. This includes skirting removal, utility disconnection, transport, setup (blocking, leveling, anchoring), and basic utility reconnection at the new site.

The cost breaks down into several components. Transport itself runs **$1,000–$2,500** for distances under 50 miles, per CostHelper (2024) and HomeGuide (2026). Setup services — blocking, leveling, and anchoring to **HUD Wind Zone I standards** (the designation for all of Bexar County, requiring standard diagonal tie spacing at 70 mph fastest-mile wind speed) — add **$2,500–$3,500**. Utility disconnection and reconnection contribute another **$500–$1,500**.

Regulatory costs are modest but mandatory. A **TxDMV single-trip manufactured housing permit costs $44.75** ($40 fee plus $4.75 processing), valid for five days on a specified route. The **TDHCA Notice of Installation (Form T) runs $75** for a single-section home, and the installer must report the installation within seven days so TDHCA can schedule an inspection — which must occur before skirting is installed. The **City of San Antonio mobile home installation permit is $98** (FY 2026 Development Services fee schedule, revised October 2025). If you need a new TDHCA Statement of Ownership, add **$55**.

San Antonio's competitive transporter market — with firms like Martinez Mobile Homes (established 1983), Texas Elite Mobile Home Transport (BBB A+, USDOT #4056947), and at least a dozen others — keeps pricing below national averages. ProMatcher's San Antonio-specific data from 2018 showed single-wide local transport at **$2,200–$2,727**, which, adjusted for inflation, aligns with the $2,500–$3,000 transport-only range in the current market. Most single-wides are 12–14 feet wide, falling under the 16-foot threshold that triggers escort vehicle requirements in Texas, which saves **$175–$350** compared to wider loads on a 50-mile move.

### 2. What is the average cost to move a double-wide mobile home in San Antonio?

The average full-service double-wide move in San Antonio costs approximately **$11,000–$12,000** for a local move under 50 miles, with the full range spanning **$7,000–$18,000** depending on distance, site conditions, and service inclusions.

Double-wides must be split into two halves for transport. Each half requires its own transport vehicle and TxDMV permit (**$89.50 total** for two sections). Texas law allows two sections traveling in convoy to share escort vehicles if they remain within 1,000 feet of each other, which reduces costs. Each half is typically 12–16 feet wide; if either section exceeds 16 feet, one escort vehicle is required on two-lane highways (positioned in front) or on four-lane highways (positioned in rear), at **$1.75–$2.00 per mile per vehicle**.

The "marriage" of two halves at the destination is the most labor-intensive phase: aligning floor systems, sealing the roof ridge, weatherproofing the center seam, and reconnecting interior electrical, plumbing, and HVAC systems across the marriage line. This process alone costs **$2,000–$4,000**. Setup (blocking, leveling, anchoring) runs approximately **$5,500** for a double-wide on concrete, per Sharp Mobile Homes' published pricing.

The **TDHCA Notice of Installation for a double-wide is $100** ($75 for the first section plus $25 for each additional section). All other San Antonio permits remain the same: **$98 city installation permit** and **$55 Statement of Ownership**. CPS Energy coordination for disconnection and reconnection is assessed on a case-by-case basis after a site inspection; their 2024 Contribution in Aid of Construction (CIAC) for new residential service connections averages approximately **$1,250**. SAWS charges a **$12 meter trip fee** for turn-on/turn-off, with a **$100 deposit** for combined water and sewer service. If a new water/sewer connection is required at the destination, SAWS impact fees range from **$7,343 to $11,528** depending on the location's elevation zone and collection area — a cost that increased approximately 23% in July 2024.

### 3. Does distance affect mobile home moving costs in Texas?

Distance is one of the three biggest cost drivers for manufactured home transport in Texas, alongside home size and service level. **Every additional mile adds $5–$15 to the total cost**, depending on home width and the number of escort vehicles required.

The pricing structure in Texas follows a base-fee-plus-per-mile model. For the first 25–50 miles, most San Antonio transporters charge a flat rate: roughly **$1,700–$2,000 per section** for transport alone. Beyond 50 miles, per-mile billing takes over. Published rate structures show single-wide towing at **$4.00–$5.50 per mile**, with escort vehicles adding **$1.50–$3.50 per mile** per car. Texas Statewide Mobile Home Transport, based in San Antonio (ZIP 78223), reported double-wide rates of **$2.50–$4.75 per mile** for distances over 100 miles on ProMatcher.

A practical illustration: moving a single-wide 25 miles within San Antonio might cost **$3,500–$5,000** full-service. The same home moved 150 miles to Austin could run **$6,000–$10,000**, driven by per-mile transport charges, additional fuel, crew per diem (**$175–$200/night** for jobs over 50 miles), and mobilization fees (**$6.00/mile** for crew/equipment travel beyond 50 miles from the transporter's base). TxDMV permits remain a flat **$44.75 per section** regardless of distance, though overweight permits add county-specific fees of **$175 for 1–5 counties** crossed, scaling up to **$1,000 for routes crossing 100+ counties**.

Texas's flat terrain through Central and South Texas keeps costs below national per-mile averages. Routes between San Antonio and Houston, Dallas, or Austin avoid mountain grades and most low-clearance obstacles. However, routes through the Texas Hill Country (northwest of San Antonio toward Kerrville or Fredericksburg) involve winding roads and elevation changes that slow transport and may require route surveys costing **$300–$1,000** for complex loads.

### 4. What is the cheapest time of year to move a mobile home in San Antonio?

**November through February offers the lowest manufactured home transport prices in San Antonio**, with savings of **10–25%** compared to peak-season rates. The absolute cheapest window is mid-November through mid-January, when demand is at its annual low and transporters are most willing to negotiate.

Summer months (May through September) are peak season for manufactured home moves across Texas, with the highest demand and prices. This pattern is driven by school-year timing, favorable daylight hours, and general real estate activity. San Antonio's extreme summer heat — regularly exceeding **100°F** from June through August — doesn't deter demand but does affect crew productivity and can soften asphalt under heavy loads.

Texas law prohibits manufactured home transport on six national holidays: New Year's Day, Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, and Christmas. TxDMV permits are valid for only **five days**, so holiday-adjacent moves require careful scheduling. Additionally, hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30; while San Antonio sits roughly 150 miles inland, tropical storm remnants can bring heavy rains and wind gusts that halt all oversize load movement.

The optimal scheduling sweet spot for San Antonio is **October through early November** or **late February through March**: moderate weather (highs in the 70s–80s), past hurricane season's peak, before spring demand ramps up, and transporter availability is high. Mid-week moves (Tuesday through Thursday) and mid-month dates typically offer additional savings of **5–10%** versus weekends or month-end dates.

Beyond timing, booking **30–45 days in advance** is critical. San Antonio-area transporters are typically booked a month out even during the off-season, and last-minute scheduling commands a premium.

### 5. Are there hidden costs when moving a mobile home in San Antonio?

Yes — and they can add **$3,000–$12,000** beyond the base transport quote if not anticipated. The most common hidden costs specific to San Antonio involve utility coordination, soil conditions, skirting, and component rentals.

**Skirting removal and reinstallation** is frequently excluded from base quotes. Removing vinyl skirting costs **$200–$500**; reinstalling new vinyl skirting at the destination runs **$1,000–$1,500** for a single-wide and **$1,400–$3,000** for a double-wide, with premium materials (faux brick, concrete) pushing costs to **$2,500–$4,000**. TDHCA requires that state installation inspections occur *before* skirting is installed, so timing this work correctly is essential.

**Axle and tire rental** catches many homeowners off guard. Older homes often lack functional axles, requiring rentals at approximately **$200 per axle** — and homes typically need 5–9 axles. Tire and wheel rentals add **$25 per tire**, with blowout replacements at **$50 per tire**. Hitch welding costs **$200–$400 per hitch**. Total running-gear rental can reach **$1,000–$2,000**.

**CPS Energy utility coordination** does not follow a simple fee schedule. Disconnection and reconnection costs are assessed after an on-site inspection, with invoices due within five business days. New service connections average approximately **$1,250** following the five-phase CIAC increase completed July 1, 2024. Separate HVAC disconnect/reconnect by a licensed technician runs **$300–$800**, and CPS Energy requires all electrical work to pass City of San Antonio inspection before energizing.

**SAWS water and sewer fees** are more transparent: **$12 meter trip fee** for turn-on/turn-off, **$35 for same-day service**, and a **$100 deposit** for combined water and sewer accounts. The major cost shock comes if you need a *new* connection: SAWS impact fees for a standard 5/8-inch residential meter range from **$7,343 to $11,528** depending on your location's elevation and sewer collection zone — these fees jumped roughly 23% in July 2024.

**Bexar County's expansive clay soil** creates hidden foundation costs unique to the San Antonio area. The region's vertisol soils, dominated by montmorillonite clay minerals, swell dramatically when wet and shrink during drought — a cycle amplified by San Antonio's pattern of 205 sunny days punctuated by heavy rain events. A **geotechnical soil test ($300–$1,000)** is strongly recommended before any manufactured home placement in Bexar County. Pier-and-beam foundations are preferred over slabs because piers can be re-leveled as soil shifts; budget **$450–$900 every 3–5 years** for periodic re-leveling. Drainage solutions (French drains, re-grading) to manage moisture around the foundation can add **$1,000–$5,000**.

**Escort and pilot car requirements** apply based on load width. Single-wides under 16 feet wide need no escort. Loads 16–18 feet wide require one escort vehicle; loads over 18 feet need two. Each escort costs **$1.75–$3.50 per mile**. Texas requires escort vehicle operators to be certified — one of only 13 states with this requirement. For a 50-mile double-wide move with two escort vehicles, this adds **$175–$350**.

Other commonly overlooked costs include **transport insurance ($300–$800)** since standard homeowners policies typically exclude coverage during transit, **steps and deck removal/reinstallation ($800–$5,000)**, **plumbing disconnect/reconnect ($300–$500 per day for a licensed plumber)**, and the **TDHCA Notice of Installation fee ($75–$125 depending on home sections)**. Route surveys for loads exceeding 20 feet wide or 19 feet tall cost **$300–$1,000** and are required by TxDOT regulations.

## Conclusion

San Antonio's manufactured home transport market benefits from structural cost advantages — flat terrain, competitive transporter supply (15+ active firms including three BBB-accredited companies), inexpensive TxDMV permitting, and Wind Zone I's standard anchoring requirements. The most actionable insight for San Antonio homeowners is that **the gap between transport-only and full-service pricing is enormous**: a single-wide transport-only move can cost as little as $1,000, but the realistic all-in cost with setup, utilities, permits, and skirting lands at $4,000–$6,000. For double-wides, the all-in range of $10,000–$14,000 reflects the inherent complexity of splitting, transporting, and remarrying two sections.

The two largest hidden cost risks specific to San Antonio are **SAWS impact fees for new connections** ($7,343–$11,528 after the July 2024 increase) and **foundation complications from Bexar County's expansive clay soil**, which can add $1,000–$5,000 in drainage and engineering work that would be unnecessary in sandy-soil regions. Homeowners who schedule moves during the November–February window, book 30–45 days in advance, and obtain itemized quotes specifying exactly which services are included will consistently land in the lower half of each price distribution.
