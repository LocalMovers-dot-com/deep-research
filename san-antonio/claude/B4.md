# Manufactured home transport pricing in San Antonio follows sharp seasonal and regulatory rhythms

**The cheapest month to move a manufactured home in the San Antonio metro is January; the most expensive is July.** Pricing swings of roughly **35–40%** separate the annual trough from peak, driven by a convergence of military PCS cycles, housing-market seasonality, daylight-dependent TxDOT regulations, severe weather disruptions, and Eagle Ford Shale workforce housing demand. Texas leads the nation in manufactured home shipments — **18,343 units in 2024**, nearly 2.5 times second-place Florida — and San Antonio sits at the intersection of nearly every demand driver that shapes this market. No transport company publishes a seasonal rate card, but triangulating TMHA shipment data, industry pricing guides, TxDOT regulatory constraints, and demand-driver analysis yields a defensible month-by-month pricing model.

## Month-by-month pricing multipliers reflect six overlapping demand cycles

The multipliers below express each month's effective transport cost relative to the annual average (1.00x baseline). They synthesize TMHA monthly shipment seasonality, general oversize-load transport pricing patterns reported by industry sources (Angi, SpareFoot, CostHelper — all 2025 editions), manufactured housing consultant guidance from the San Antonio/New Braunfels market, TxDOT daylight-hour constraints, and the specific local demand drivers detailed in subsequent sections. Manufactured Housing Consultants of New Braunfels — within the San Antonio–New Braunfels MSA — explicitly states that **"winter months from January through March offer the best deals"** due to reduced demand and inventory-clearance incentives.

| Month | Multiplier | Pricing pressure | Key demand dynamics |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **January** | **0.80x** | **Lowest** | Post-holiday lull; New Year's Day transport blackout; shortest daylight window (~10.5 hrs in SA); TMHA shipments at annual low (1,312 in Jan 2026); dealers clearing prior-year inventory; minimal PCS activity; Eagle Ford crews on reduced schedules |
| **February** | 0.83x | Very low | Continued winter lull; ice/freeze risk halts moves (Uri-type events); ~11 hrs daylight; Presidents' Day not a TxDOT blackout but slows scheduling; lowest transporter utilization of the year |
| **March** | 0.90x | Low-moderate | Spring buying season begins; daylight crosses 12 hrs; TMHA shipments tick up (~1,498 in Mar 2025); SA tornado/hail risk emerging; early PCS orders issued; Eagle Ford spring drilling ramp starts |
| **April** | 0.98x | Near-baseline | SA severe weather season peaks (April 28, 2021: **$3.7 billion** hail event); housing market accelerating; daylight ~13 hrs; PCS orders ramping; storm-damage relocations begin; some FEMA pre-positioning activity |
| **May** | 1.12x | High | PCS peak begins (JBSA processes **~4,500 HHG shipments** May–Aug); Memorial Day blackout compresses scheduling; longest non-summer daylight (~14 hrs); peak housing transaction season; Eagle Ford fully staffed |
| **June** | 1.18x | Very high | PCS peak intensifies; hurricane season opens (Gulf staging begins); **~14.25 hrs daylight** (near annual max); highest monthly manufactured home shipments in TX; transporters at capacity; FEMA may activate Beeville staging area |
| **July** | **1.20x** | **Highest** | Independence Day blackout removes peak-demand day; maximum daylight (~14 hrs); PCS peak + housing peak + hurricane season compound; fuel prices at seasonal highs; SA-area transporters report longest lead times (30–45 days); TMHA 12-month shipment moving average peaked July 2025 |
| **August** | 1.15x | High | PCS tail-end surge (school-year deadline); back-to-school housing placements; hurricane season active (Beryl landfall July 8, 2024 strained capacity into August); daylight shortening but still >13 hrs |
| **September** | 1.02x | Slightly above baseline | Labor Day blackout; hurricane season peak (historically highest Gulf storm probability); FEMA MHU deployment risk; PCS winding down; demand begins seasonal decline |

| **December** | 0.82x | Very low | Christmas Day blackout; shortest daylight (~10.3 hrs); year-end inventory overhang (14,700 unsold units statewide as of Mar 2025); holiday scheduling gaps; cold-weather delays; TMHA shipments at second-lowest point |

**Cheapest month: January (0.80x).** Demand hits its structural floor — holiday hangover, minimum daylight, frozen PCS activity, and dealer inventory pressure all converge. A double-wide move priced at $12,000 baseline might cost **~$9,600** in January.

**Most expensive month: July (1.20x).** The same move could run **~$14,400** — a **$4,800 spread** from peak to trough. PCS demand, maximum daylight utilization, hurricane-season transporter diversion, and Independence Day scheduling compression all stack simultaneously.

## FEMA deployments and severe weather commandeer transporters at unpredictable intervals

San Antonio occupies one of the most weather-exposed positions in the manufactured home transport market. The metro sits in "Flash Flood Alley," borders Tornado Alley, and serves as a primary federal disaster-staging corridor for Gulf Coast hurricanes. Severe weather affects transport pricing through two channels: **direct damage requiring emergency relocations** and **transporter diversion to FEMA contracts**.

**Hurricane Harvey (August 2017)** remains the benchmark disruption. FEMA projected **8,500 manufactured housing units** for deployment to Texas survivors — later determined to be an overestimate by ~2,600 units, costing **$152.4 million in unnecessary procurement, transport, and storage** (DHS OIG Report OIG-20-15, February 2020). The Beeville staging area, a former naval base **90 miles southeast of San Antonio** and one of only three national FEMA disaster staging areas, stored approximately **2,400 single- and double-wide homes plus 200 additional trailers/RVs**. Transporting thousands of MHUs from factories and national storage sites (Cumberland, MD; Selma, AL; Sherwood, LA) to Texas, then distributing them to individual survivor sites, absorbed enormous manufactured home transport capacity. DAT Solutions reported **spot-market truckload rates jumped 6 percentage points** post-Harvey; fuel costs spiked **15% nationally** because Texas refineries (representing ~30% of U.S. capacity) temporarily shut down.

**Hurricane Beryl (July 2024)** followed a different pattern. Making landfall as a Category 1 between Matagorda and Galveston on July 8, 2024, Beryl generated FEMA disaster declaration DR-4798 covering **121 affected counties** with **$750+ million** approved for nearly 700,000 households. However, FEMA relied primarily on Transitional Sheltering Assistance (hotel-based) rather than large-scale MHU deployment, reducing direct transporter diversion. Gulf Coast manufactured home values still dropped **18% below 2023 levels** from insurance premium spikes.

**San Antonio's hail and tornado exposure** is extreme in its own right. The metro area recorded **222 ground-level hail reports** from trained spotters in a recent 12-month period and **79 severe weather warnings**. Texas sustained **$6.5 billion** in hail property damage from 2012–2021 (highest nationally). The **April 28, 2021 hailstorm** caused **$3.7 billion in damages** across central Texas. In May 2025, a confirmed tornado near I-10 in Bexar County damaged homes and businesses across the metro. NOAA data shows manufactured home residents are **15–20 times more likely to die in tornadoes** than occupants of permanent structures, and EF-1 winds can completely destroy a manufactured home — generating urgent emergency transport demand when homes must be relocated or replaced.

The **June 2025 San Antonio floods** (record **3.98 inches/hour** rainfall; 13 killed; joint disaster declaration) and the catastrophic **July 4, 2025 Hill Country floods** (Guadalupe River rose 26 feet in 45 minutes; **135+ killed** in Kerr County) added a flooding dimension to the severe-weather transport disruption profile. **Winter Storm Uri (February 2021)** demonstrated cold-weather vulnerability: all 254 Texas counties were affected, road closures shut down transport statewide, and 500,000 insurance claims were filed — though burst pipes rather than structural destruction meant limited MHU deployment.

## The JBSA military PCS cycle concentrates demand into a 16-week window

Joint Base San Antonio is the **largest concentration of military installations in the Department of Defense**, with **67,350 direct full-time employees** including **32,333 active-duty personnel** across Lackland AFB, Randolph AFB, Fort Sam Houston, and Camp Bullis (Texas Comptroller, 2023). The base generates an estimated **$55.1 billion** in total economic output and supports a total workforce impact of **240,403 jobs** including indirect employment.

During peak PCS season (May through August), JBSA processes approximately **4,500 household goods shipments** — roughly 60–65% of annual PCS volume compressed into 16 weeks. With service members rotating every 2–3 years, JBSA generates an estimated **10,000–16,000 PCS-related moves annually** (inbound and outbound combined). The manufactured home component is small but structurally significant: DoD regulations (USTRANSCOM DTR Part IV, Attachment K5) allow service members to ship a manufactured home **in lieu of household goods**, though the regulation itself warns that "experience shows that few mobile homes are moved without exceeding prescribed entitlements." Given that approximately **6–7% of Texas housing stock is manufactured** and military families skew toward budget-constrained junior enlisted personnel, an estimated **1–3% of JBSA PCS moves** may involve manufactured home transport — translating to roughly **100–480 manufactured home moves annually** directly attributable to military PCS.

The indirect effect is larger. Multiple manufactured home communities cluster near JBSA installations — **Lackland Mobile Home & RV Park**, **Military Highway Mobile Home Park** (bordering Lackland's perimeter), **Ridge Crest MHC** (10–20 minutes from Lackland), and **Hidden Lake Mobile Home Park** (1.5 miles from Fort Sam Houston's Martindale Army Airfield). MHVillage lists **18+ manufactured home communities** near Fort Sam Houston alone. Military families cycling in and out of these communities every 2–3 years create steady manufactured home buying, selling, and repositioning demand throughout the PCS season — even when the home itself isn't transported cross-country.

A major policy shift looms: on **May 28, 2025, the Pentagon ordered military services to cut discretionary PCS budgets by 50% by FY2030** (10% by FY2027, 30% by FY2028, 40% by FY2029, 50% by FY2030). If implemented at JBSA proportionally, this could reduce annual PCS-driven transport demand by thousands of moves over the next four years.

## Population growth and housing affordability fuel structural transport demand

San Antonio added approximately **23,945 residents** in 2023–2024, ranking **fourth nationally** for numeric population growth. The city now claims roughly **1.57 million residents** (surpassing Philadelphia to become the **6th largest U.S. city** by early 2026), while the metro area approaches **2.8 million**. From April 2020 to July 2024, the metro gained nearly **205,000 people** — **59% from domestic migration** (~121,000 from other states, led by California, Florida, and New York relocations). Bexar County alone absorbed **49,946 in-migrants** from other states in 2022.

This growth feeds directly into manufactured home transport demand. Texas received **18,343 manufactured home shipments in 2024** — more than the next two states combined (Florida at 7,405 and North Carolina at 6,768). Every shipped home requires transport from factory to dealer, then dealer to homesite. Multi-section homes — now comprising over half of Texas shipments (722 of 1,312 in January 2026) — each require **multiple transport trips**. Proportionally, the San Antonio metro likely absorbs **1,600–1,800+ manufactured homes annually**, generating an estimated **3,000–4,000+ individual transport trips** when multi-section logistics are included.

The affordability gap drives this demand. San Antonio's **median site-built home** sells for approximately **$290,000–$310,000** (2025). A new manufactured home in Texas averages **$86,700 for a single-section** and **$146,900 for a double-section** (2024 Census Bureau MHS data) — representing **58–70% savings** before land costs. San Antonio already has the **most affordable housing among Texas's four major metros**, requiring just **$89,864** in household income to afford a median home versus $138,781 in Austin. This affordability positioning makes manufactured housing a natural entry point for the roughly 24,000 new residents arriving annually, many from high-cost markets.

However, the market is currently in cyclical contraction. TMHA forecasts **15,882 Texas shipments** for 2026 (down from 18,343 in 2024), with dealer inventory at **14,700 unsold units statewide** — nearly double the 7,900 in mid-2022. Factory-gate prices for new multi-sections fell from **$134,700 in 2023** to **$114,200 by Q1 2025** (15% nominal decline). Industry participants expect a mid-2026 rebound.

## Eagle Ford Shale makes San Antonio a workforce housing staging hub

San Antonio serves as the primary staging and supply hub for manufactured workforce housing deployed to Eagle Ford Shale oilfield areas. The Eagle Ford extends south and east of San Antonio across roughly 38 counties, with core drilling counties — **Webb, DeWitt, Karnes, La Salle, Dimmit** — all within **60–120 miles** of the city via I-35 and connecting highways.

Several San Antonio-based companies explicitly market oilfield workforce housing transport. **Mi Casa Housing** (San Antonio) builds, transports, and installs workforce units across the Eagle Ford, Permian Basin, and Bakken, maintaining its own trucks and translifts. **Manufactured Housing Consultants** (San Antonio) lists "Oil Field Housing" as a dedicated product category. **Texas Built Mobile Homes** (Seguin, 35 miles east of SA) specializes in scalable workforce configurations up to 10-bedroom dormitories for both Eagle Ford and Permian Basin operations.

Eagle Ford rig counts reveal the demand cycle's volatility: **~95 rigs pre-pandemic** (2019), crashing to **just 10 in August 2020**, recovering to **71 by March 2023**, then settling to **~55 by early 2024** as operators consolidated. The basin produced **1.147 million barrels/day** in March 2024, and ConocoPhillips' Marathon Oil acquisition positions the Eagle Ford as a "major growth driver." Critically, the Eagle Ford has the **fewest drilled-but-uncompleted (DUC) wells** of any major U.S. shale basin (347 as of March 2024), meaning future production requires new drilling and sustained workforce presence — not just completions of existing wells.

The Permian Basin (centered 300+ miles west around Midland/Odessa) has its own localized supply chain, though SA-based companies like Mi Casa Housing and Texas Built do serve it for manufactured housing wholesale and long-haul transport. San Antonio's role is more dominant for the Eagle Ford, where the geographic proximity makes it the natural logistics hub.

## Park closures and infrastructure displacement create forced-move demand spikes

A landmark **January 2020 UT Austin School of Law report** — "Endangered: San Antonio's Vanishing Mobile Home Parks and a Path for Preservation" — documented that **9 mobile home parks closed since 2014**, with **5 condemned in 2018 alone** through proactive city inspections. Approximately **88 active parks** remained, housing **~22,000 residents** in **~8,000 mobile homes** with a median household income of **$36,000**.

The defining displacement case was **Mission Trails Mobile Home Park** (1515 Mission Road), where City Council voted in 2014 to rezone the property for a $75-million luxury apartment development. **~300 residents** were displaced and **106 mobile homes** had to be relocated — the **largest residential displacement in San Antonio since Hemisfair Park in the 1960s**. A 2017 study by Vecinos de Mission Trails documented devastating consequences: **1 in 4 displaced households experienced homelessness**; nearly half reported new or worsened health problems; relocation payments ranged from just $5,600 to $7,500 per household.

The UT Law report notes that many older mobile homes "would break apart if moved," meaning park closures generate transport demand only for structurally sound newer units while destroying housing for the remainder. In a positive counter-example, **Riverside Terrace** residents formed a co-op in November 2023 and purchased their 46-unit park for **$3 million** with $3.9 million in city support, preserving it as Mission Trail Community Association.

The major infrastructure projects — **I-35 NEX** ($1.5 billion central phase, $852 million south phase) and **Loop 1604 North expansion** ($1.4 billion, 23 miles) — were engineered to minimize displacement. TxDOT chose elevated lanes fitting within existing right-of-way specifically because of "extensive development along frontage roads." **Port San Antonio** and **Brooks** redevelopments displaced former military housing (site-built), not manufactured home communities. The transport demand from displacement is therefore driven primarily by **code enforcement closures and private redevelopment** rather than infrastructure projects.

## Winter Texan snowbird migration barely registers in San Antonio's transport market

San Antonio is emphatically **not** a Winter Texan destination. The seasonal "snowbird" phenomenon is overwhelmingly concentrated in the **Rio Grande Valley** (McAllen, Mission, Harlingen, Brownsville), which hosts approximately **100,000 Winter Texans** (57,000 households per UTRGV's 2022–23 survey) across **200+ dedicated RV and manufactured home resort communities**. The RGV Winter Texan economy generates **$761 million in direct spending** and **$1.9 billion** in total economic impact annually.

San Antonio's manufactured home communities are **year-round permanent-resident affordable housing**, not seasonal resorts. No SA-area community was found that specifically markets to or accommodates Winter Texans. The Hill Country between San Antonio and Austin does attract some seasonal visitors, but they arrive in personal RVs — not transported manufactured homes. The seasonal arrival pattern (October–November) and departure (March–April) with peak occupancy January–March has **no measurable impact** on SA-area manufactured home transport demand.

Where San Antonio does rank highly is as a **permanent retirement destination** — **#2 nationally** for retiree in-migration (SmartAsset 2024) — driven by no state income tax, housing costs 21% below national averages, and "Military City USA" identity attracting ~250,000 veteran retirees. But these permanent relocations involve conventional home purchases, not seasonal manufactured home transport.

## TxDOT travel windows create de facto day-of-week pricing tiers

Texas Transportation Code Chapter 623, Subchapter E, and TxDMV administrative rules (43 TAC Chapter 219) impose travel restrictions that effectively tier scheduling costs by day and time, even though weekend moves are technically permitted.

**Daylight-only movement** is the binding constraint. Manufactured homes may travel only during **30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset**. This means a December move in San Antonio has roughly **10.3 usable hours** versus **14.25 hours in June** — a **38% reduction** in daily transport capacity. Winter moves may require additional travel days for the same distance, increasing per-move costs through crew time, overnight staging, and permit duration. Single-trip permits are valid for a maximum of **5 days** at **$40 per permit**.

**All manufactured housing transport is prohibited on six national holidays** (New Year's Day, Memorial Day, Independence Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, Christmas) — a stricter standard than general oversize loads, which restrict only loads exceeding certain dimensions. This removes **six high-demand scheduling days** from the calendar, compressing moves into adjacent periods and creating scheduling premiums around holiday weeks.

**Urban curfew windows** restrict travel through San Antonio's metro core, typically during **6:00–9:00 AM and 3:00–6:00 PM** rush hours, as specified on each individual permit. This effectively reduces usable urban transport hours by 6 hours daily, forcing either early-morning departures or midday-only urban transits.

**Weekend transport is permitted** during daylight hours with no blanket restriction. However, weekend moves carry a **practical premium** for several reasons: escort vehicle operators (required for homes over 16 feet wide at **$1.50–$3.50 per mile each**) charge overtime rates; TxDOT permits must be obtained in advance; and crew availability is constrained. Industry sources indicate weekend premiums of roughly **10–20%** are common though not universal. **Weather halts** — movement is prohibited when road surfaces are hazardous from rain, ice, wind, or other conditions — add unpredictable cost during spring storm season and winter freeze events.

The net effect: **midweek moves during off-peak months** (particularly Tuesday through Thursday in January or February) represent the lowest-cost scheduling window. **Weekend moves during peak summer** (especially weeks bracketing the July 4 blackout) represent the highest-cost window — combining peak seasonal demand, holiday compression, and overtime labor premiums.

## Conclusion

San Antonio's manufactured home transport market is shaped by an unusually dense concentration of demand drivers that stack, compound, and occasionally collide. **Six distinct forces** — PCS military cycling, population-growth-driven new placements, FEMA disaster deployments, Eagle Ford workforce housing, park displacement relocations, and the TxDOT regulatory calendar — each follow their own seasonal rhythm but converge most intensely from May through August. The **0.80x-to-1.20x pricing range** from January trough to July peak reflects real capacity constraints: the same finite pool of specialized transporters, escort vehicles, and permitted daylight hours must absorb military PCS surges, summer housing placements, potential hurricane deployments, and oilfield restocking simultaneously.

The most actionable insight for cost-conscious movers: **schedule for January or February, midweek, and lock in quotes 45–60 days ahead.** The most dangerous pricing environment: **post-hurricane landfall in July or August**, when FEMA contracts can commandeer transporter capacity regionwide while PCS and housing-market demand remain at annual highs. The Pentagon's planned 50% PCS reduction by FY2030 could structurally soften the May–August peak in coming years, but the population-growth and severe-weather drivers show no signs of abating.
